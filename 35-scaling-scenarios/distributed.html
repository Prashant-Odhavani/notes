Distributed Failure Scenarios

Five failures that only exist once there's a network in the middle. Two of them are made worse by the obvious fix.

🔴 Advanced ⭐ Senior-level
Quick revision
  • Slow is worse than dead. A dead dependency fails fast; a slow one exhausts you.
  • Timeouts first, then retries, then a circuit breaker. In that order of importance.
  • Retries without backoff and jitter turn a blip into an outage.
  • At-least-once is the norm. Duplicates are a design input, not a bug.
  • Idempotent consumers give you an exactly-once effect.
  • Ordering is per-partition at best — design for out-of-order instead.
  • Lag trend matters, not depth. Growing is the problem; large but flat isn't.
  • Compensation is not rollback. It's a new business action.

Slow is worse than dead — and this is the most important idea here

A dependency that refuses connections fails in milliseconds. Your request fails fast, the thread is released, you return an error, and the system stays healthy while one feature is broken.

A dependency that accepts connections and responds in 30 seconds is far more damaging. Every in-flight request holds a thread and a connection for 30 seconds. Requests accumulate. Your thread pool is exhausted. Now every endpoint is slow, including ones that don't touch that dependency at all.

One slow dependency has taken down your entire service. That's the cascading failure, and it's why a partial outage becomes a total one.

Which makes the ordering of controls clear. Timeouts matter most, because without one there's no bound on the damage — and .NET's HttpClient default of 100 seconds is far too long for a service-to-service call. Two seconds is often right.

Then a circuit breaker, so you stop paying the timeout on every request once it's clearly down — fail immediately instead, and probe occasionally to see if it recovered.

Then a bulkhead, capping concurrent calls to that dependency, so even without a breaker it can only ever consume a slice of your capacity rather than all of it.

The general principle: bound every wait. Anything unbounded is a queue that can grow until it takes the process with it.

builder.Services.AddHttpClient<IPricingClient, PricingClient>(c =>
{
    c.BaseAddress = new Uri(cfg["Pricing:BaseUrl"]!);
    c.Timeout = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(5);        // overall ceiling, NOT the default 100s
})
.AddResilienceHandler("pricing", b => b
    // Order matters: this pipeline runs outside-in
    .AddConcurrencyLimiter(permitLimit: 20, queueLimit: 10)   // bulkhead
    .AddRetry(new HttpRetryStrategyOptions
    {
        MaxRetryAttempts = 3,
        BackoffType = DelayBackoffType.Exponential,
        UseJitter = true,                        // ESSENTIAL - see below
        ShouldHandle = new PredicateBuilder<HttpResponseMessage>()
            .Handle<HttpRequestModel>()
            .HandleResult(r => r.StatusCode >= HttpStatusCode.InternalServerError
                            || r.StatusCode == HttpStatusCode.RequestTimeout)
            // Do NOT retry 4xx - the request is wrong, retrying cannot help
    })
    .AddCircuitBreaker(new HttpCircuitBreakerStrategyOptions
    {
        FailureRatio = 0.5,                      // half of requests failing
        MinimumThroughput = 20,                  // over a meaningful sample
        SamplingDuration = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(30),
        BreakDuration = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(15)
    })
    .AddTimeout(TimeSpan.FromSeconds(2)));       // per attempt
Jitter, and why retries make outages worse without it

Exponential backoff alone doesn't solve the problem it appears to. If a thousand clients all fail at the same moment — because the dependency restarted — they all wait one second, then all retry simultaneously. Then all wait two seconds and retry simultaneously.

You've replaced continuous load with synchronised spikes, which is often worse: the dependency recovers, gets hit by a thousand simultaneous requests, falls over again, and the cycle repeats. That's a retry storm, and it can keep a recovered service down indefinitely.

Jitter randomises each delay, so the retries spread out and the dependency sees a manageable ramp instead of a wall. It's a single flag and it's the difference between a self-healing system and a self-perpetuating outage.

Two related rules. Never retry a 4xx — the request is malformed or unauthorised, so retrying cannot succeed and just adds load. And be careful retrying at multiple layers: three retries in the client inside three at the gateway inside three in the caller is 27 requests for one logical call. Retry at one level, deliberately chosen.

Senior signal — exactly-once delivery doesn't exist; exactly-once effect does

The framing that matters: you cannot have exactly-once delivery across a network, because the acknowledgement can always be lost. If the consumer processes a message and then crashes before committing, the broker has no way to know the work was done — so it must either redeliver (at-least-once) or risk losing it (at-most-once).

Every real system picks at-least-once, because losing messages is worse than repeating them. Which means duplicates are a design input, not a defect.

So the goal isn't preventing duplicates — it's making them harmless. An idempotent consumer plus at-least-once delivery gives an exactly-once effect, which is what anyone actually wanted.

Three ways to get there, in order of preference:

Make the operation naturally idempotent. "Set status to Shipped" is safe to repeat; "increment the counter" is not. Where you can choose, choose the absolute form over the relative one — this is free and needs no infrastructure.

Deduplicate on a message id. Record processed ids and skip repeats. The important detail: the id record and the work must be in the same transaction, or you can do the work, fail to record the id, and repeat it anyway. And the id must come from the producer, not be generated on receipt.

Use a conditional write. UPDATE ... WHERE Status = 'Pending' — the second attempt affects zero rows and is a no-op. This is the same atomic-conditional-write primitive that appears in every store, and it's the most robust option because the database enforces it.

Scenario: events arrive out of order 🔥 Must know

ApproachGivesCosts
Partition by entity keyOrder within one entityParallelism limited by key distribution
Service Bus sessionsOrdered processing per sessionOne consumer per session at a time
Version or sequence numberDiscard anything older than appliedConsumer must track versions
Idempotent, commutative operationsOrder stops matteringConstrains what events can express
Buffer and reorderStrict orderingLatency, memory, and a gap may never fill
Version-based rejection beats enforcing order

Guaranteeing global ordering is expensive: it means a single partition, one consumer, no parallelism, and a head-of-line blocking problem where one slow message stalls everything behind it.

The cheaper and more robust approach is to make out-of-order arrival detectable and harmless. Every event carries a version or a timestamp from the source; the consumer stores the last version it applied and discards anything older.

So an OrderUpdated at version 5 arriving after version 7 is simply dropped — correct final state, no coordination required, full parallelism retained.

Two caveats worth knowing. This works for state replacement ("the order now looks like this") and not for deltas ("add 5 to the total"), which need every event exactly once in order. That's a strong argument for designing events as state rather than as increments.

And a create arriving after an update still needs handling — usually by upserting rather than requiring the row to exist, so a late create doesn't overwrite newer data and an early update doesn't crash.

The instinct to reach for is: can I make ordering not matter? That's almost always cheaper than enforcing it.

Scenario: consumer lag is growing 🔥 Must know

1  Is lag GROWING or just LARGE?
      Large and flat  -> a backlog draining. Fine, estimate the catch-up time.
      Growing         -> arrival rate exceeds processing rate. Act.

2  ALL partitions or ONE?
      All  -> genuine capacity, or a shared dependency slowed down
      ONE  -> skew, or a stalled/poisoned consumer on that partition

3  Did the ARRIVAL rate change, or the PROCESSING rate?
      Arrival up      -> a producer changed, a retry storm, a backfill
      Processing down -> a slow dependency, a deployment, GC, throttling

4  Can I add consumers?
      Only up to the PARTITION COUNT. One reader per partition
      per consumer group is a hard ceiling.
Senior signal — one partition lagging is a completely different problem

Aggregate lag is the metric people watch and it hides the most common case. Lag on one partition while the others are healthy rules out a capacity problem entirely — if the consumer were simply too slow, every partition would lag.

So it's one of two things.

Skew. The partition key concentrates traffic — one large tenant, one misbehaving device. Same hot-partition physics as everywhere else, and adding consumers cannot help, because there's a hard limit of one reader per partition per consumer group. You can only reduce the events landing there or speed up processing per event.

A stalled consumer. Usually a poison message: the handler throws on a specific event, the code retries or rethrows, and that partition makes no progress while everything else continues. On Event Hubs and Kafka there's no dead-letter queue, so a single bad message can hold up a partition indefinitely. The fix is to record the failure elsewhere and move on.

A third, easily missed: ownership churn. If the processor is rebalancing repeatedly — instances scaling up and down, or the checkpoint store being throttled — a partition spends its time re-establishing ownership rather than processing. Worth checking the checkpoint storage account specifically, because it's a different resource and nobody looks at it.

Which is why the metric to have is lag per partition, not aggregate. An average across 32 partitions hides exactly this.

Scenario: a multi-step operation failed halfway 🔥 Must know

ResponseWhen
Retry the failed stepIt's transient and the step is idempotent. Prefer this
Compensate completed stepsThe operation cannot proceed and effects must be undone
Leave it and alertAutomatic resolution is riskier than a human deciding
Complete it laterEventual completion is acceptable — persist and resume
Compensation is not rollback

A database rollback makes it as though nothing happened. Compensation cannot do that — the earlier steps really happened, and the world may have observed them.

So compensating a payment isn't "un-charging" it; it's issuing a refund, which is a separate business event with its own record, its own fees, and its own appearance on the customer's statement. The customer sees a charge and a refund, not nothing.

That has design consequences people miss:

Compensations can fail too, so they need retries, and eventually a human — an operation stuck half-compensated is worse than either state.

Compensation must be idempotent, because the retry will happen.

Some steps cannot be compensated at all. An email has been sent; a webhook has fired. So order the saga to put irreversible steps last, once everything reversible has succeeded.

And there's no isolation: another process can observe the intermediate state. If that's unacceptable, the pattern is a pending status — the record exists but isn't treated as real until the saga completes. That's the semantic version of a lock, and it's what makes eventual consistency tolerable to the rest of the system.

Which is why the honest advice is: if the steps can live in one transaction, put them in one transaction. A saga is what you use when they genuinely can't.

Key takeaways
  • Slow is worse than dead — one slow dependency can exhaust the whole service.
  • Timeouts matter most; HttpClient's 100s default is far too long.
  • Then a circuit breaker, then a bulkhead. Bound every wait.
  • Backoff without jitter creates synchronised retry spikes.
  • Never retry a 4xx; don't stack retries at multiple layers.
  • Exactly-once delivery is impossible; exactly-once effect is achievable.
  • Prefer naturally idempotent operations; dedupe in the same transaction as the work.
  • Conditional writes are the most robust deduplication.
  • Version-based rejection beats enforcing global order.
  • Design events as state, not deltas, so order stops mattering.
  • Lag trend matters, not depth; one partition lagging is skew or a stall.
  • Consumers cannot exceed the partition count.
  • Compensation is a new business action, must be idempotent, and can fail.
  • Put irreversible saga steps last; use a pending status for visibility.

Common mistakes

  • No timeout, so a slow dependency exhausts the thread pool.
  • Retries without jitter, producing synchronised storms.
  • Retrying 4xx responses.
  • Retries at three layers, multiplying load 27×.
  • Assuming exactly-once delivery.
  • Deduplication outside the transaction that does the work.
  • Events as increments, so a duplicate corrupts state.
  • Enforcing global ordering when version rejection would do.
  • Alerting on queue depth rather than its trend.
  • Adding consumers beyond the partition count and expecting throughput.
  • Rethrowing on a poison message, stalling a partition.
  • Treating compensation as rollback.
  • Irreversible steps early in a saga.
  • A circuit breaker with no fallback, so open means total failure.

Interview questions

Advanced

A downstream service starts responding in 30 seconds instead of 200ms. What happens to you? 🔥 Must know
How I'd answer

"Without protection, my service goes down entirely — and the important part is that it fails worse than if the dependency had simply died."

"The mechanism: every request calling that dependency holds a thread and a connection for 30 seconds. Requests keep arriving, so in-flight work accumulates. The thread pool is exhausted, and now every endpoint is slow — including ones that never touch that dependency. A partial outage has become a total one."

"That's why slow is worse than dead. A dependency refusing connections fails in milliseconds: the thread is released, I return an error, one feature is broken and the rest of the service is fine. Failing fast is a feature."

"It gets worse if retries are involved. If each request retries three times with a 30-second timeout, that's 90 seconds per request and triple the load on a dependency that's already struggling — so my retries are helping to keep it down."

"What I'd want in place, in order of importance:"

"A timeout. The single most important control, because without one there's no bound on the damage. And I'd specifically flag that HttpClient's default is 100 seconds, which is catastrophic for a service-to-service call — two seconds is often right. If the dependency normally answers in 200ms, waiting 30 seconds serves nobody: the user has gone."

"A circuit breaker. Once a threshold of requests is failing, stop calling it and fail immediately. Otherwise every request still pays the full timeout, so I'm slow rather than fast-failing — and I'm still generating load. The breaker probes occasionally to detect recovery."

"A bulkhead — a concurrency limit on calls to that dependency. That's the one that contains the blast radius structurally: even with no breaker, that dependency can only ever consume 20 of my threads rather than all of them. Other endpoints stay healthy regardless."

"Retries with exponential backoff and jitter, and jitter matters more than people think. Without it, a thousand clients that failed simultaneously all retry simultaneously — so the dependency recovers, gets hit by a wall of requests, falls over again. Jitter spreads them into a ramp. It's one flag and it's the difference between self-healing and a self-perpetuating outage."

"A fallback where the feature allows it — cached or stale data, a default, or a degraded response. A circuit breaker with no fallback still means total failure for that feature; with one it means degraded service, which is usually much better than an error page."

"Diagnosing it live, I'd look at dependency duration and failure rate in the telemetry to confirm which dependency, and thread pool queue length to confirm the exhaustion. The signature is rising latency across unrelated endpoints, which is what tells me it's cascading rather than local."

"Immediate mitigation if the protections aren't there: reduce the timeout — which can be config rather than a deploy — or disable the feature by flag, or trip the breaker manually. Getting off that dependency restores everything else."

"The architectural question I'd raise afterwards: does this call need to be synchronous at all? If the result isn't needed to answer the request, publishing to a queue and processing asynchronously removes this failure mode completely — the dependency being slow becomes a growing queue rather than an outage. That's the structural fix rather than better handling of a bad situation."

What they're really checking
  • Do you know slow is worse than dead, and why?
  • Do you know the HttpClient default timeout?
  • Do you order timeout, breaker, bulkhead correctly?
  • Can you explain why jitter matters?
  • Do you notice retries add load to a struggling dependency?
  • Do you question whether the call should be synchronous?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: "add retries" as the main answer.
  • Red flag: no mention of timeouts.
  • Red flag: assuming only the dependent feature breaks.
Likely follow-ups
  • What timeout would you pick, and how?
  • How do you choose circuit breaker thresholds?
  • What if the call genuinely must be synchronous?
Customers report being charged twice. Diagnose and fix. ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"This is a correctness incident affecting money, so I'd handle it in a specific order: stop it getting worse, quantify who's affected, fix them, then fix the cause. Not root-cause first."

"First, stop the bleeding. If it's ongoing and I can identify the path, disable it by feature flag or take that route out of service. A payment path producing duplicates should not keep running while I investigate — the cost of downtime is much lower than the cost of more double charges."

"Second, quantify. Query for multiple successful charges against the same order or the same customer within a short window. I need the exact list, because refunding is a customer-communication exercise and 'we think about fifty people' isn't good enough."

"Third, remediate. Refund the duplicates and contact those customers before they contact us. That matters commercially — a proactive refund is a very different experience from a disputed charge, and a chargeback costs more than the refund."

"Then the cause, and there are four candidates I'd distinguish."

"A user double-click or double-submit. Two requests, both processed. The fix is a client-generated idempotency key sent with the request, so the second is recognised as the same logical operation. Disabling the button is a UX nicety, not a fix — the request can always arrive twice."

"A client or gateway retry. The charge succeeded but the response was lost — a timeout, a dropped connection — so the client retried. This is the common one, and it's important because nothing was broken: the payment provider behaved correctly, we behaved correctly, and the outcome is still wrong. That's why idempotency is a design requirement rather than a bug fix."

"Message redelivery. If the charge happens in a message handler, the lock expired mid-processing, or the consumer crashed after charging and before completing the message. The broker redelivers because it has no way to know the work was done — that's at-least-once, working as designed."

"Concurrent requests racing. Two requests both check 'has this been charged?', both see no, both charge. A check-then-act race, and it's the one that survives every naive fix because the window is small enough to look impossible."

"The fix, and I'd want it at multiple levels."

"Idempotency keys. The client generates a key per logical payment attempt and sends it. We store it with the result. A repeat with the same key returns the original result rather than charging again."

"The critical detail: a unique constraint on the key in the database, not an application-level check. A check-then-insert is exactly the race above. Insert first and let the second attempt fail on the constraint — then the database enforces it atomically, and no amount of concurrency defeats it. Same reasoning as a conditional write in any store: make the database the arbiter, not your code."

"Pass the key to the payment provider. Stripe and most others support idempotency keys natively, so even if our logic fails, the provider deduplicates. That's defence in depth, and it's free."

"A conditional state transition. UPDATE Payments SET Status='Charging' WHERE Id=@id AND Status='Pending' — if it affects zero rows, someone else is already handling it. That's an atomic claim, and it's the pattern I'd reach for generally."

"Prevention beyond the fix. A test that asserts submitting the same payment twice concurrently charges once — because that's the case nobody tests, and it's the one that fails. An alert on multiple charges per order per hour, so the next occurrence is detected in minutes rather than by customers. And I'd audit for the same check-then-act shape elsewhere, because if it's here it's probably in registration, in invitations, and anywhere else a uniqueness rule is enforced in code."

"The general lesson I'd state: at-least-once delivery is normal, so any operation with a real-world side effect needs an idempotency mechanism enforced by the database. Preventing duplicates from arriving is not achievable; making them harmless is."

What they're really checking
  • Do you stop the bleeding and remediate customers before root-causing?
  • Do you distinguish double-submit, retry, redelivery and a race?
  • Do you know a check-then-insert is itself a race?
  • Do you enforce idempotency with a database constraint?
  • Do you pass the key to the provider as well?
  • Do you look for the same pattern elsewhere?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: disabling the button as the fix.
  • Red flag: an application-level "already charged?" check.
  • Red flag: debugging first, leaving the path live.
Likely follow-ups
  • Where does the idempotency key come from?
  • How long do you retain keys, and what expires them?
  • What if the provider charged but our write failed?
