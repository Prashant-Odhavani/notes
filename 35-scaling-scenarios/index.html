Scaling Scenarios

"Your API got ten times the traffic and it's falling over. What do you do?" These questions test method, not memory — so this page is the method.

🔴 Advanced ⭐ Senior-level
Quick revision
  • Characterise before diagnosing. What exactly is slow, for whom, since when?
  • Percentiles, never averages. An average hides the tail users complain about.
  • Separate mitigation from fix. Stop the bleeding, then find the cause.
  • Does it scale with load? That one question splits queueing from a slow path.
  • Measure before changing anything. Guessing under pressure makes it worse.
  • State what you'd look at — naming the metric matters more than naming the cause.
  • Say what you'd do differently next time, structurally.

Senior signal — a wrong hypothesis with the right method beats the reverse

Candidates often think the goal is to identify the cause quickly. It isn't. The interviewer knows the answer already; what they're learning is how you'd behave during a real incident.

So "it's probably an N+1 query" as an opening is a weak answer even when it turns out to be right, because it's a guess. And a real incident where you act on a guess costs you the time you spend on the wrong thing, plus the risk of a change that makes it worse.

A strong answer sounds like: "First I'd want to know whether it's all requests or a subset, and whether it started at a deployment. Then I'd look at dependency duration in the telemetry to see whether the time is in our code or downstream."

That demonstrates the two things that actually matter: you narrow before you commit, and you know which signal answers which question.

It also gives the interviewer something to engage with. They can say "the telemetry shows one slow SQL call", and now you're having a real conversation rather than being marked against a checklist.

The method 🔥 Must know

1  CHARACTERISE   What exactly is wrong? Which endpoints, which users, since when?
                  All requests or a tail? Constant or load-dependent?

2  LOCATE         Where is the time or the failure? In our code, a dependency,
                  the platform, or queueing before we even start?

3  HYPOTHESISE    Name a specific cause that would explain ALL the evidence,
                  including anything that looks contradictory.

4  VERIFY         Find the signal that confirms or kills it. Cheaply.
                  If it takes an hour to check, find a cheaper test first.

5  MITIGATE       Restore service. Scale out, disable a feature, cache, roll back.
                  Explicitly a holding action - say so.

6  FIX + PREVENT  Address the cause. Then make the class of problem
                  detectable or impossible.
The single most useful question: does it scale with load?

If you only get to ask one thing, ask this — it splits the space in half.

Latency rises with concurrency → something is queueing. A connection pool, a lock, a thread pool, a CPU limit being throttled, a single-threaded consumer. The resource is finite and contended, and adding load makes the wait longer.

Latency is constant regardless of load → a slow path. A missing index, an N+1, a slow third-party call, an expensive serialisation. It was always this slow; you've only just noticed, or volume made it matter.

Those need completely different responses. Scaling out helps the second and can worsen the first — more instances competing for the same 100 database connections makes contention worse, not better.

Which is why "scale it out" as a first move is a red flag: it's the correct fix for exactly one of the two shapes, and it's actively harmful for the other.

Percentiles, and why averages lie

A 200ms average can be 90% of requests at 50ms and 10% at 1.5 seconds. The 10% is what generates complaints, and the average conceals it entirely.

So p50, p95 and p99. The gap between them is the information: p50 close to p95 means everything is uniformly slow; p50 fast with p99 terrible means a subset of requests hits a different path — a cache miss, a large tenant, a cold start.

Two related points worth having ready.

Averages of percentiles are meaningless. You can't average p95 across instances and get the real p95, so a dashboard doing that is misleading — the underlying distributions have to be combined.

The tail is what users experience, because a single page load makes many requests. If each has a 1% chance of being slow, a page making 100 calls is almost certainly slow. That's why p99 matters far more than it intuitively should.

Senior signal — aggregates hide the shapes that matter

Three of the rows above are the same point, and it's worth stating as a principle: the aggregate is almost never the number you need.

Averaged across instances, one broken instance serving errors looks like a small error rate rather than a third of users failing.

Averaged across partitions, one hot Event Hubs partition with growing lag disappears into 31 healthy ones.

Averaged across tenants, one customer whose data volume broke a query looks like mild degradation.

In every case the aggregate is reassuring and wrong. So the diagnostic habit is to split by dimension early — instance, partition, tenant, endpoint — because a problem confined to one value of a dimension has a completely different cause from one spread across all of them.

Which is also the argument for enriching telemetry with tenant id before you need it. You cannot retrospectively split by a dimension you never recorded.

Say which one you're doing, and why

The strongest thing you can do in these answers is label your own actions: "that's a mitigation, it doesn't fix the cause, and here's what I'd do afterwards."

It shows you know the difference, and it's what actually happens in incidents — you restore service first and investigate properly afterwards, because a running system buys you the time to think.

The failure modes at either extreme:

Only mitigating. Scale up, move on, and it recurs at the next growth step — with the cost now permanent. Enough of these and the system is expensive and fragile.

Only root-causing. Debugging carefully while users are down, when a rollback would have restored service in two minutes. During an incident, restoring service beats understanding it.

One caution worth voicing: preserve the evidence before you mitigate. Capture a dump, grab the logs, note the metrics — a restart or a scale-out often destroys exactly the state you need to diagnose it, and then it recurs a week later with nothing to go on.

Senior signal — recognising the pattern is most of the answer

These ten patterns cover the large majority of production scaling problems, across every technology in this handbook. That's the useful realisation: the same small set of physics keeps reappearing in different clothing.

A hot partition in Cosmos DB, a hot key in Redis, a skewed partition key in Event Hubs and a sequential blob name prefix are one problem. The API differs; the reasoning and the fix — increase key cardinality, because adding capacity cannot help — are identical.

So when you meet an unfamiliar scenario, the productive move is to ask which pattern this is, rather than reaching for technology-specific knowledge you may not have.

And it's genuinely persuasive in an interview to say it out loud: "this looks like a hot partition — one unit saturated while the others are idle, and I'd expect adding capacity not to help. Same shape as a hot key in Redis." That demonstrates transferable understanding rather than memorised cases, which is exactly what distinguishes seven years of experience from one year repeated seven times.

Ask questions — the scenario is deliberately underspecified

"The API is slow" is not enough information to diagnose anything, and it's incomplete on purpose. The interviewer is waiting to see whether you notice.

Good questions, roughly in order of value: all endpoints or specific ones? all users or one tenant? when did it start, and did anything deploy? is it constant or load-dependent? what do the p95 and p99 look like?

Candidates skip this because it feels like stalling. It's the opposite — it's the single clearest signal that you've done this for real, because nobody who has handled an incident starts by proposing fixes.

It also makes the rest of the conversation much better: once you know it started at 14:00 and only affects one endpoint for one tenant, you're solving an actual problem rather than reciting a checklist.

And finish with prevention. "How would you have known sooner?" is frequently the real question, and often unasked — an alert on the metric that would have caught it, or a structural change that makes the failure impossible, is what separates a good answer from a complete one.

Key takeaways

  • Characterise, locate, hypothesise, verify, mitigate, fix and prevent.
  • "Does it scale with load?" splits queueing from a slow path.

Common mistakes

  • Proposing a fix before characterising the problem.
  • "Scale it out" as the first move, when contention is the cause.
  • Reasoning about averages instead of percentiles.
  • Looking only at aggregates, so a single instance or tenant is invisible.
  • Not asking a single clarifying question.
  • Naming a cause with no way to verify it.
  • Confusing mitigation with fix, or doing only one of them.
  • Restarting or scaling before capturing evidence.
  • Adding retries to a struggling dependency without backoff.
  • Ignoring prevention, so the same class recurs.

Interview questions

Advanced

Walk me through how you'd approach a production performance problem. 🔥 Must know
How I'd answer

"I'd work through it in a fixed order, because the main risk in an incident is acting on a guess and spending your time on the wrong thing — or making it worse."

"First, characterise it. 'The API is slow' isn't diagnosable, so I want to know: is it all endpoints or specific ones? All users or one tenant? When did it start, and did anything deploy around then? Is it constant, or does it get worse with load? And what do p95 and p99 look like, not the average."

"That last distinction matters — a 200ms average can be 90% of requests at 50ms and 10% at a second and a half, and the 10% is what people are complaining about. The gap between p50 and p99 tells me whether everything is uniformly slow or a subset hits a different path."

"The single most useful question is whether latency scales with load. If it rises with concurrency, something is queueing — a connection pool, a lock, the thread pool, a CPU limit being throttled. If it's constant regardless of load, it's a slow path — a missing index, an N+1, a slow third party."

"Those need opposite responses, which is why I wouldn't lead with 'scale it out'. Scaling helps a slow path; with contention it can make things worse, because more instances compete for the same finite database connections."

"Second, locate the time. I'd compare total request duration against dependency duration in the telemetry. If dependencies account for most of it, the problem is downstream and I'd look at which one. If they don't, it's in-process — CPU, serialisation, GC, or contention."

"I'd specifically check the number of dependency calls per operation, because an N+1 shows up as hundreds of 2ms calls with nothing individually slow. No latency percentile reveals that; the dependency count does."

"And I'd split by dimension early — by instance, and by tenant. If it's one instance, that's a completely different problem from all of them: a bad node, a corrupted pool, an instance that's GC-thrashing. If it's one tenant, it's usually data volume exposing a missing index. Aggregates hide both, and they're reassuring while being wrong."

"Third, form a hypothesis that explains all the evidence — including anything that looks contradictory, because that's usually the most informative part. Low CPU with high latency, for instance, points at throttling or thread pool starvation rather than expensive work."

"Fourth, verify cheaply before committing. If confirming it takes an hour, I'd look for a faster test first."

"Then mitigate, and I'd say explicitly that it's a mitigation. If it started at a deploy, roll back — that's usually fastest and safest. Otherwise scale, disable a feature flag, or shed load. But I'd capture evidence first — a dump, the logs, the metrics — because a restart destroys exactly the state I need, and then it recurs next week with nothing to go on."

"Then the real fix, with the trade-off stated. And finally the part I think matters most: how would we have known sooner? Usually an alert on the specific metric that moved first, and where possible a structural change so the class of problem can't recur — a test that fails on an N+1, a quota that stops one tenant affecting others."

"One framing I'd add: most of these turn out to be one of a small set of recurring patterns — hot key, queueing on a finite resource, thundering herd, N+1, local state on multiple instances, unbounded growth. Recognising which pattern it is gets you most of the way, and it transfers across technologies. A hot partition in Cosmos, a hot key in Redis and a skewed Event Hubs partition key are the same problem with different APIs — and in all three, adding capacity doesn't help."
