Behavioural + Technical

These aren't a soft round. At senior level they're where the actual hiring decision gets made — and they're the part most engineers prepare least.

🟡 Intermediate ⭐ Senior-level
Why these matter more than the technical rounds at seven years

Everyone shortlisted for a senior role can write the code. What separates candidates is whether they can be trusted with decisions, disagreements and other people — and that's what these questions probe.

They're also where a strong technical candidate most often falls over, because they're improvised. Someone who has prepared their architecture answers carefully will then ramble through "tell me about a conflict" and leave an impression of vagueness.

The fix is straightforward: prepare four stories properly and know them well enough to tell in two minutes. Not scripted, but structured — you should know the impact, the specific decision you made, and what you'd do differently.

One habit that changes the quality of every answer: at senior level, talk about influence rather than output. "I built X" is a mid-level answer. "I built X, and got two other teams to adopt it, which meant Y" is a senior one — same work, and it demonstrates the thing they're actually assessing.

Structuring a story 🔥 Must know

  SITUATION    ~15%   Just enough context. Two sentences.
  TASK         ~10%   What YOU specifically owned.
  ACTION       ~50%   What you DECIDED and why, including the alternatives
                      you rejected. This is the substance.
  RESULT       ~25%   The outcome, with a number if you have one, AND what
                      you'd do differently.

  The two failures:
    - 80% situation, 20% everything else. Nobody needs the org chart.
    - "we" throughout, so it is unclear what YOU did. Use "I" for your
      decisions and "we" for team outcomes - deliberately.
The action section is where the signal is

Most people over-invest in setup and under-invest in the decision. The interviewer doesn't need the team structure or the project's history — they need to know what you decided, what you considered instead, and why you chose as you did.

So the most valuable sentence in any of these answers is usually: "the alternative was X, and I chose Y because…". That single construction demonstrates judgement rather than activity, and it's what distinguishes a story from an anecdote.

Two more things that lift an answer. A number in the result, even an approximate one — "deployment went from a weekly two-hour ritual to on-demand" is much stronger than "it improved things". And what you'd do differently, volunteered rather than extracted, because it shows you reflected afterwards rather than moved on.

And be careful with pronouns. Systematic "we" makes it impossible to tell what you contributed, which is the most common way a good story lands weakly. Use "I" for your decisions and "we" for the team's outcome — that's both accurate and clearer.

The four stories to prepare 🔥 Must know

StoryAnswers questions about
1. A hard technical decision Judgement, trade-offs, architecture, "a decision you regret"
2. A production incident you handled Ownership, debugging, pressure, communication, prevention
3. A disagreement with a colleague Conflict, influence, being wrong, disagree-and-commit
4. Something you improved for other people Initiative, mentoring, influence without authority, impact
Four stories cover almost every question asked

Behavioural questions are numerous and they map onto a small set of underlying assessments. So four well-prepared stories, told from different angles, cover the ground — and preparing four properly is far more effective than preparing twelve superficially.

"Tell me about a failure", "a decision you regret", "a time you had to choose between speed and quality" can all draw on story 1. "Working under pressure", "a time you took ownership", "how you handle being on call" all draw on story 2.

The trick is to know each story well enough to emphasise the part the question asked about rather than telling it identically each time. Same events, different weighting.

Story 4 is the one people don't have, and it's the most senior-signalling of the four — because improving things for other people is what the role actually is. It doesn't need to be impressive: a test suite you made reliable, documentation that reduced repeated questions, a build you made fast, a junior you brought up to speed. Impact on others is the point, not scale.

The questions, and what they're really asking 🔥 Must know

Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision. 🔥 Must know
What they're assessing, and how to answer

"They're checking three things: whether you can disagree without being difficult, whether you can be wrong gracefully, and whether you can commit to a decision you lost. The third is the one they care most about, and the one most answers skip."

"So the shape I'd use: what the disagreement was, how I tried to resolve it rather than win it, what the outcome was, and what happened afterwards."

"The most useful thing to describe is how you found the real disagreement. Two people can agree on every technical fact and differ on which risk matters, or on an unstated assumption about volume or lifespan. Asking 'what would have to be true for your approach to be clearly right?' usually surfaces it."

"Then: did you try to make it empirical rather than rhetorical? A prototype, a benchmark, production data. Performance arguments especially are worth measuring rather than debating, because intuition is unreliable there."

"And reversibility as a tie-breaker — if it can't be resolved, take the decision that's easier to undo. That reframes it from 'who's right' to 'what's the cheapest way to find out', which is more productive and demonstrably senior thinking."

The strongest version of this story is one where you turned out to be wrong. It's counterintuitive and it's true: describing how you changed your mind when shown a real problem answers all three assessments at once, and it's much rarer than a story where you were right.

If you were right, the important part isn't the vindication — it's that you committed properly to the alternative while it was being tried, rather than half-implementing it and waiting to be proven correct.

What to avoid: a story where the other person was simply incompetent. It answers a different question, unflatteringly — an interviewer hears how you'll talk about their team later. Describe the other position as reasonable, because it usually was from where they stood.

Red flags in the answer
  • Red flag: "I was right and they eventually realised."
  • Red flag: escalating immediately rather than trying to resolve it.
  • Red flag: no commitment after the decision went the other way.
  • Red flag: winning by seniority rather than by reasoning.
Tell me about a mistake you made. 🔥 Must know
What they're assessing, and how to answer

"They want to know whether you can own something without either deflecting or over-apologising, and whether you extracted something transferable."

"Pick a real mistake with real consequences. A fake-humble one — "I care too much about quality" — is transparent and wastes the question. Equally, it doesn't need to be catastrophic: a well-analysed medium-sized mistake beats a dramatic one told badly."

"The structure: what happened, the impact in concrete terms, what you did about it immediately, and the systemic change that came out of it."

"That last part is what separates answers. "I'll be more careful" isn't a fix. The senior version identifies why the mistake was possible and removes that possibility — a check in CI, a guardrail, a required review, an alert. If your prevention relies on someone remembering, you've learned the shallow lesson."

"For example: rather than "I deleted production data and now I double-check", the stronger version is "I deleted production data, and it was possible because we all had write access to production — so we moved to read-only access with a PIM-elevated break-glass path, and the class of mistake is now structurally prevented"."

"Own it cleanly. "The requirements were unclear" is a deflection even when true — the senior response is that clarifying them was part of your job. And don't over-flagellate either; excessive self-criticism reads as poor calibration rather than as humility."

"One thing worth including if it applies: how you told people. Reporting your own mistake promptly is a strong signal, and the instinct to hide it is common enough that the contrast lands.

Red flags in the answer
  • Red flag: a fake mistake, or one with no consequence.
  • Red flag: blaming requirements, process, or someone else.
  • Red flag: "I'll be more careful" as the prevention.
  • Red flag: excessive self-criticism.
How do you handle competing priorities, or a deadline you can't meet? ⭐ Senior-level
What they're assessing, and how to answer

"Whether you communicate early, whether you can say no constructively, and whether you understand that prioritisation isn't yours to do alone."

"The core of a good answer: the moment you know a deadline is at risk, that's the moment to say so. Not when it arrives. Early warning gives other people options — cutting scope, adding help, moving the date, informing a customer. Late warning gives them none, and that's what makes it a failure of communication rather than of estimation."

"Then: come with options rather than a problem. "We won't make it" is a report. "We can make it with these two features cut, or make it complete two weeks later, or ship for one customer segment on time — here's what I'd recommend" is a decision someone can actually take."

"On competing priorities, the senior move is to surface the conflict rather than absorb it. If two stakeholders both need something first, quietly working extra hours to do both hides a real prioritisation decision from the people whose job it is to make it — and it isn't sustainable, so it fails later and more expensively."

"Saying no constructively is worth articulating: not "no", but "yes, and here's what moves" or "not this sprint, and here's when". That keeps you a collaborator rather than an obstacle while still protecting the commitment you've already made."

"And I'd mention what I'd protect under pressure: cutting scope rather than quality, and specifically not compromising anything expensive to change later — the data model, a public contract — or anything creating a security or data-integrity risk. Having a line and being able to name it is the point."

Red flags in the answer
  • Red flag: "I work longer hours" as the primary answer.
  • Red flag: raising the risk only when the deadline arrives.
  • Red flag: deciding priorities unilaterally.
  • Red flag: cutting quality silently to hit a date.
How do you approach mentoring, or code review? ⭐ Senior-level
What they're assessing, and how to answer

"Whether you make other people better, which at senior level is a substantial part of the job — and whether your review style would be pleasant to be on the receiving end of."

"On code review, the things worth saying:"

"Distinguish blocking from non-blocking comments. A reviewer who leaves twenty comments of equal apparent weight is exhausting and the important one gets lost. Marking something as a preference rather than a requirement is a small habit that changes the experience a lot."

"Explain why, not just what. "Use X instead" teaches nothing; "use X because Y fails under concurrency" means they'll get it right next time without me."

"Ask rather than assert when you might be missing context. "What happens if this is called twice?" is better than "this isn't idempotent" — partly because it's collaborative, and partly because sometimes there's a reason you don't know about."

"Automate the mechanical. Formatting, naming conventions, analyzer rules — a human arguing about brace placement is wasted review capacity. Humans should review design and correctness."

"And "split this up" is a legitimate comment. Review quality collapses with size — a 2,000-line change gets skimmed, so it receives less scrutiny than its risk warrants. People are oddly reluctant to ask for smaller pull requests."

"On mentoring, the framing I'd offer: the goal is for them not to need you, which means pairing on something hard rather than answering the question, and letting them make reversible mistakes rather than preventing every one. Someone who has debugged something painful themselves has learned something that can't be transferred by explanation."

"Worth mentioning the AI angle if it comes up: I'd be careful that fluent generated output doesn't substitute for the struggle that builds judgement. Using it to explain and to check your work accelerates learning; using it to produce code in an area you can't yet evaluate doesn't."

Red flags in the answer
  • Red flag: review as gatekeeping or fault-finding.
  • Red flag: arguing about style rather than automating it.
  • Red flag: mentoring described as telling people the answer.
  • Red flag: no interest in making others better.
Why are you looking to leave? 🟡 Intermediate
What they're assessing, and how to answer

"This looks like small talk and it isn't. They're assessing how you'll talk about them later, and whether what you want is something this role actually offers."

"So: frame it as moving toward something, not away. "I want to work on systems at a scale I haven't yet" or "I want more architectural ownership" is forward-looking and specific."

"Being honest but measured about a negative is fine and often better than pretending everything is perfect. "The product is mature and mostly maintenance now, and I want to build things again" is credible. What doesn't work is criticising individuals, or describing the company in terms that make you sound bitter — an interviewer hears that as a preview."

"The strongest version connects to this specific role. If you know something about their domain, their scale or their technology, saying why that's what you're looking for makes the answer about fit rather than about escape."

"And keep it brief. This isn't the question to spend four minutes on — a couple of sentences, forward-looking, then move on."

Red flags in the answer
  • Red flag: criticising individuals by role or name.
  • Red flag: a list of grievances.
  • Red flag: "money", as the only reason.
  • Red flag: a reason this role also wouldn't fix.

Senior signal — your questions are part of the assessment

Asking "what's the tech stack?" late in a process signals you haven't been paying attention. Asking "how long does a change take to reach production?" signals you know that number predicts almost everything else about how a team works.

The two most informative questions in my view are that one and "what happens when something breaks at 3am?" The first tells you about automation, testing and release confidence. The second tells you about on-call load, whether incidents lead to blame or to fixes, and whether the team has the observability to diagnose anything.

"What's the most frustrating part of the codebase?" is worth asking because the manner of the answer matters as much as the content. A candid answer suggests a healthy culture; a defensive or evasive one is itself information.

And genuinely listen to the answers — you're deciding too. A vague answer to "what would you want me to have achieved in six months?" usually means nobody has thought about it, which tends to predict how the first six months will actually go.

Key takeaways
  • At senior level these rounds carry the decision, and they're the least prepared.
  • Prepare four stories: hard decision, incident, disagreement, something you improved for others.
  • Weight the action section — the decision and the rejected alternative are the signal.
  • "The alternative was X, and I chose Y because…" is the most valuable sentence available.
  • Use "I" for your decisions and "we" for team outcomes, deliberately.
  • Include a number, and volunteer what you'd do differently.
  • Talk about influence, not just output.
  • The strongest disagreement story is one where you turned out to be wrong.
  • Never describe the other person as incompetent — it answers a different question.
  • A mistake's prevention must be structural; "I'll be more careful" is the shallow lesson.
  • Raise deadline risk the moment you know, and bring options rather than a problem.
  • Surface competing priorities rather than absorbing them.
  • Review: separate blocking from preference, explain why, automate the mechanical, ask for smaller PRs.
  • Mentoring aims at them not needing you.
  • Frame leaving as moving toward something, briefly.
  • Ask how long a change takes to reach production, and what happens at 3am.
