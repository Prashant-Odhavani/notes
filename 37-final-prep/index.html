Final Interview Prep

The last stretch. Less about learning new material, more about being able to say what you already know, clearly and out loud.

🟡 Intermediate 🔥 Must know

The one thing that matters most 🔥 Must know

Knowing and explaining are different skills

You can understand the outbox pattern perfectly and still explain it badly under pressure — hesitating, starting in the wrong place, missing the point that makes it click.

The interview tests the second skill. And it's the one that improves fastest with practice, because it's a performance rather than a body of knowledge.

Senior signal — practise out loud, not by reading

Reading an answer produces a feeling of competence that doesn't survive being asked. The gap between "I know this" and "I can say this in ninety seconds, starting with the right sentence" is large, and reading never closes it.

So the highest-value preparation is saying answers aloud — to a colleague, to a recording, or to an empty room. It's uncomfortable and it works, because it forces you to discover where you actually hesitate.

Three specific things it fixes. It reveals that you don't know where to start — most weak answers are well-informed and badly ordered. It surfaces the topics you've read about versus understood, because you can't fake a causal explanation out loud. And it builds the fluency that makes you sound like someone who has done the work rather than someone who revised it.

Every "How I'd answer" block in this handbook is written to be spoken, in roughly the order you'd say it. That's deliberate: they're scripts to practise, not text to memorise. Say them in your own words, and when you can do that without looking, that topic is done.

Spend your time on the gaps, not on what you enjoy

The natural instinct is to revise what you're already good at, because it feels productive. It's the least useful thing you can do.

A quick way to find real gaps: go through Rapid Revision and, for each line, try to say the why out loud. Not the fact — the mechanism. "EF Core caches the model" is recall; "so a query filter capturing the tenant id by value bakes one tenant's id in for everyone" is understanding. Anywhere you can state the fact but not the consequence is a gap.

Then read only those pages properly. Six well-understood topics beat thirty skimmed ones, because an interviewer will follow up on whatever you raise — and a topic you mentioned but can't go one level deeper on is worse than not raising it.

And a note on the last day: don't learn new material. It won't be retrievable under pressure and it displaces the consolidation that would have helped. Revise what you know, sleep properly, and arrive calm.

Senior signal — the difference is depth on failure, not breadth on features

The clearest separator between one year of experience repeated seven times and seven years of experience is what you say about failure.

Anyone can describe how a feature works. Someone who has run it in production says what happens when it breaks: that a slow dependency is worse than a dead one, that at-least-once delivery means duplicates are a design input, that a liveness probe checking the database restarts every pod at once, that adding capacity never fixes a hot key.

None of those are advanced concepts. They're consequences, and knowing consequences is what experience actually is.

So when you have a choice about what to say, choose the failure mode. "We'd use a queue" is a component. "We'd use a queue so a traffic spike grows a backlog instead of timing out — the cost being that the caller gets a 202 and needs a way to check status" is the same knowledge, delivered as judgement.

That reframing costs nothing and changes how the whole answer reads. It's the single most transferable habit in this handbook.

Read Red Flags even if you read nothing else here

Most preparation focuses on knowing more. Red Flags covers the opposite risk: answers that are confidently wrong, or technically true and revealing.

"Microservices for scalability", "we'd add a distributed lock", "we'd increase the memory limit", "async makes it faster" — each sounds like the right answer and each tells an experienced interviewer something unflattering.

Those are cheap to fix, because you already know the correct version. The problem is purely that the wrong phrasing is the one that comes out under pressure — so recognising them in advance is worth more per minute than almost anything else in this section.
