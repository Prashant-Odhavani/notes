Senior-Level Questions

These have no textbook answer. They're asking whether you've owned something long enough to have learned what it costs.

🔴 Advanced ⭐ Senior-level
What these questions are actually probing

A mid-level engineer can answer "how does X work?" as well as a senior one. The separator is questions where the answer requires having lived with a decision.

Three shapes recur: "when would you not use X?", "what would you do differently now?", and "walk me through a time it went wrong."

All three are unanswerable from documentation, which is exactly why they're asked. And all three reward the same thing: naming a specific cost you paid.

The failure mode isn't ignorance — it's answering them abstractly. "It depends on the requirements" is true and empty. A specific example, even a small one, is worth more than a general principle stated well.

"When would you not use it?" 🔥 Must know

When would you not use microservices? ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"Most of the time, honestly — and I'd start from what they actually buy, because the case for them is narrower than it's usually presented."

"Microservices have one primary benefit: independent deployability. Team A ships without coordinating with team B. Everything else attributed to them is either achievable in a monolith or is a liability: a monolith scales horizontally perfectly well, technology diversity is mostly a hiring problem, and fault isolation is real but you've added network failures that didn't exist before."

"So I wouldn't use them when independent deployability isn't the bottleneck. A single team of eight deploying together gains nothing and pays for network calls that fail, no transactions across boundaries, distributed tracing, versioned contracts, and eventual consistency they now have to explain to the business."

"Specifically I'd avoid them before the domain boundaries are understood. Getting a boundary wrong in a monolith is a refactor; getting it wrong across services is a migration with data movement. And you rarely know the right boundaries early — which is the strongest argument for waiting."

"What I'd do instead: a modular monolith with boundaries enforced mechanically — an architecture test that fails the build if one module references another's internals. Then splitting later is mechanical rather than archaeological, because the seams already exist and are already enforced."

"The failure mode I'd name is a distributed monolith: services that share a database, or that can't be deployed independently because a change requires three of them to ship together. That's all the cost with none of the benefit, and it's the most common outcome of splitting too early."

"If I'm asked when I would: several teams needing independent release cadence, a component with genuinely different scaling characteristics — an ML inference service, say — or a regulatory requirement to isolate a workload. Those are real."

What they're checking
  • Do you know the one real benefit, and that scalability isn't it?
  • Do you connect team structure to the decision?
  • Do you know a distributed monolith is worse than either?
  • Can you name when you would, so it isn't just contrarianism?
When would you not use a message queue? ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"When the caller needs the answer to continue. If the user is waiting on the result, a queue just adds a hop and a polling mechanism — you've made it slower and given yourself two systems to debug."

"Also when the operation must be strongly consistent with something else. If reserving stock and creating an order must be atomic, putting a queue between them replaces a transaction with a saga: compensation logic, eventual consistency, and partial-failure states. That's a lot of complexity for no gain."

"And when ordering matters and the queue can't give it without serialising everything. Getting ordered processing usually means one consumer per session or partition, at which point you've traded the throughput you added the queue for."

"The cost I'd name from experience is debuggability. A synchronous call that fails gives you a stack trace. An asynchronous flow that fails gives you a message somewhere, a consumer that may have retried, and a dead-letter queue — and answering 'what happened to this request?' requires distributed tracing you have to have set up in advance."

"Plus the operational surface: a dead-letter queue nobody monitors, consumer lag nobody alerts on, and poison messages. Each is a thing you now have to run."

"Where a queue genuinely earns it: work that doesn't need a synchronous answer, load levelling so a traffic spike grows a backlog instead of timing out, fan-out to several consumers, and retry with durability for something that must not be lost. Those are strong reasons — I'd just want one of them to be true rather than reaching for it by default."

What they're checking
  • Do you know async isn't automatically better?
  • Do you recognise that a queue between two things breaks their transaction?
  • Do you name the debuggability and operational cost?
When would you not use event sourcing or CQRS? ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"I'd separate them first, because they're routinely conflated. CQRS is separating the read and write models. Event sourcing is storing state as a sequence of events. You can do either without the other, and CQRS is much cheaper."

"CQRS comes in levels. Separate models over one database is nearly free — often just different classes for commands and queries, and I'd reach for it whenever reads and writes genuinely want different shapes. A separate read store buys scale at the cost of eventual consistency, and that's where the real decision is."

"So I'd skip CQRS when the read and write models are basically the same. Adding two models for a CRUD screen is pure overhead."

"Event sourcing I'd avoid unless the history genuinely is the domain. It's a substantial commitment: projections to build and rebuild, eventual consistency on every read, event schema evolution that must stay readable for years, and a team that has to understand it. Onboarding cost is real — most developers haven't worked with it."

"The specific trap: events are permanent. A poorly designed event is with you for the lifetime of the system, and you can't migrate it the way you'd migrate a table. So you need to be good at domain modelling before you start, which is a lot to ask early in a project."

"Where it's genuinely right: a regulated domain where you must reconstruct what you knew and decided at a past moment, or a domain where the transitions themselves are the valuable data — claims progression, trading, anything auditable."

"And the middle path I'd propose: event sourcing for one bounded context where it earns its cost, not for the whole system. Or, cheaper still, bi-temporal versioned rows plus decision records — which satisfies most audit requirements while staying queryable with plain SQL."

What they're checking
  • Do you know CQRS and event sourcing are different?
  • Do you know CQRS has cheap and expensive forms?
  • Do you raise the permanence of events and the onboarding cost?
  • Do you offer a proportionate middle path?

"What would you do differently?" 🔥 Must know

Tell me about an architectural decision you regret. ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer — the structure, with your own example

"The shape that works here is: the decision, why it was reasonable at the time, what it actually cost, and what I'd do now. The middle part matters — a regret with no defensible original reasoning reads as poor judgement rather than learning."

"Something like: 'We split a service earlier than we understood the domain. It seemed right because two teams were working in the same codebase and stepping on each other.'"

"'What it cost: the boundary was wrong. Orders and inventory needed to be consistent, and we'd separated them — so the core purchase flow became a saga with compensation. Every incident afterwards involved orders in a half-completed state, and we spent months building tooling to inspect and repair them.'"

"'What I'd do now: keep them in one service and enforce module boundaries in the codebase instead — an architecture test failing the build on cross-module references. That would have solved the actual problem, which was teams colliding in shared code, without buying a distributed-systems problem.'"

"'The lesson I took: draw boundaries from what must be transactional together, not from team structure. And the number of sagas in a design is a good signal that the boundaries are wrong.'"

"What makes this land is the specific cost. 'It was more complex' is vague. 'We spent months building repair tooling for half-completed orders' is a fact, and it's the part that shows you lived with it."

"I'd also be careful not to make it someone else's fault. Owning a decision that turned out badly is the point of the question — blaming the previous team answers a different one, unflatteringly."

What they're checking
  • Can you criticise your own work without either defensiveness or excessive self-blame?
  • Was the original decision defensible? (If not, that's a different concern.)
  • Is the cost specific and quantified?
  • Did you extract a transferable principle?
Prepare two of these in advance

One technical, one about process or people. This question is asked in some form almost every time, and improvising it produces either a non-answer ("I can't think of one") or an over-disclosure.

How do you decide what technical debt to pay down? ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"I'd frame it as interest rate rather than principal. Some debt costs you every single change; some sits there costing nothing. The ugly module nobody touches is cheap debt — leaving it is a rational decision, not neglect."

"So the things I'd prioritise are where the debt is compounding:"

"Anything that slows every change — a brittle test suite, a build that takes forty minutes, a deployment that requires manual steps. That's a tax on all future work, so paying it down returns continuously."

"Anything that risks an incident — no retry on a critical dependency, unbounded queries, a missing index that will bite at the next growth step. That's debt with a tail risk rather than a cost."

"Anything blocking work already planned. If the next quarter's roadmap runs through a module, refactoring it becomes part of that work rather than a separate ask — which is also the easiest version to get approved."

"And what I'd deliberately not prioritise: code that's ugly but stable and rarely touched, or consistency for its own sake. Rewriting something that works to match a newer pattern is often the most expensive form of debt paydown, because you take real regression risk for no functional gain."

"On getting it approved — which is usually the harder half — I'd express it in outcomes rather than cleanliness. Not 'this code is bad' but 'this is why the last three estimates were wrong' or 'this is the third incident from the same cause'. Linking debt to something the business already feels — velocity, incidents, on-call load — is what makes it fundable."

"And I'd prefer continuous small payments over a big-bang rewrite. Rewrites have a poor record: they take longer than estimated, they reintroduce fixed bugs, and they stop feature work for months. Improving the module you're already touching is lower-risk and doesn't need permission."

What they're checking
  • Do you prioritise by ongoing cost rather than by aesthetics?
  • Do you know some debt is correctly left alone?
  • Can you make the business case in business terms?
  • Are you appropriately sceptical of rewrites?

"Tell me about a time it went wrong" 🔥 Must know

Walk me through the worst production incident you've handled. ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer — the structure

"I'd tell it in a fixed order, because the order is what demonstrates method: impact, detection, diagnosis, mitigation, root cause, prevention."

"Impact first, in user terms rather than technical ones. 'Checkout was failing for about 40% of customers for 25 minutes' rather than 'the pods were restarting'. That shows you think about consequence before mechanism."

"Detection — and be honest here. If a customer told us before monitoring did, say so; that's a finding, and admitting it is more credible than implying perfect observability."

"Diagnosis, including the wrong turn. 'We initially thought it was the database because queries were timing out, then realised the queries were fine and requests were queueing before reaching it.' Real diagnosis has a wrong turn in it, and including one makes the story believable."

"Mitigation, labelled as mitigation — 'we rolled back, which restored service in four minutes; that didn't fix the cause'. And ideally: 'we captured a dump first, because a restart would have destroyed the evidence and we'd have been guessing next time'."

"Root cause, with the actual mechanism. Not 'a bad deployment' but 'a sync-over-async call added on a hot path, which held pool threads for the whole I/O — so under load requests queued and CPU stayed low, which is why it looked like a database problem'."

"Prevention, and this is the part that separates answers. Two levels: the specific fix, and the structural change so the class of problem can't recur. 'We fixed the call, and we enabled the analyzer that fails the build on sync-over-async, and we added an alert on thread pool queue length.'"

"The habit I'd want to convey: a fix that relies on nobody making that mistake again isn't a fix. And if the incident revealed a monitoring gap, the alert is as much a deliverable as the code change."

"I'd also mention communication if it applied — who was updated and how often. At senior level, handling the stakeholders during an incident is part of the job, and a lot of candidates omit it entirely."

What they're checking
  • Impact before mechanism?
  • Honest about detection and about wrong turns?
  • Mitigation distinguished from root cause?
  • Evidence preserved before restarting?
  • Prevention structural, not procedural?
  • Did you communicate with anyone?

Judgement under constraint ⭐ Senior-level

You have two weeks and the "right" solution takes two months. What do you do? ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"First I'd want to know why two weeks — because the answer changes what's reasonable. A regulatory deadline, a contractual commitment and 'someone picked a date' are three different situations, and only the third is negotiable."

"Then I'd look for a smaller version of the right shape rather than a rushed version of the full thing. Those are very different. Cutting scope keeps the design sound; cutting quality creates debt with compounding interest."

"Concretely: can we ship for one customer segment, or one region, or with a manual step where the automation would go? That's a real product in two weeks, with the architecture pointing the right way."

"What I'd protect even under pressure: anything expensive to change later. Data model, public API contract, partition key, and anything touching money or personal data. Those are the decisions you can't cheaply revisit, so they're the wrong place to save a week."

"What I'd happily compromise: internal code quality, some test coverage on stable paths, manual instead of automated operations, a simpler UI, missing edge cases. All of those are cheap to improve later."

"And I'd be explicit about the trade-off in writing, not just verbally: what we're not doing, what it will cost to add later, and the risks we're accepting. Partly so the decision is informed, and partly so it's a shared decision rather than something engineering silently absorbed."

"The thing I'd push back on is a two-week solution presented as permanent. Temporary solutions become permanent by default — so I'd want either scheduled follow-up work or an explicit acknowledgement that this is what we're keeping, which changes what corners are acceptable."

"And I'd say what I wouldn't compromise at all: anything creating a security or data-integrity risk. Shipping without tenant isolation, or with a race that double-charges customers, isn't a faster version of the work — it's a different, worse outcome. That's the line I'd hold."

What they're checking
  • Do you interrogate the deadline?
  • Do you cut scope rather than quality?
  • Can you name what's expensive to change later?
  • Do you document the trade-off?
  • Do you have a line you won't cross?
A colleague disagrees strongly with your design. How do you handle it? ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"I'd start by trying to find the actual disagreement, because it's often not the one being argued. Two people can agree on every technical fact and differ on which risk matters more, or on an assumption neither has stated."

"So: 'what would have to be true for your approach to be clearly right?' That usually surfaces the real difference quickly — and often it's an assumption about volume, or team size, or how long the thing will live."

"If we're weighing different risks, I'd try to make it empirical rather than rhetorical. Can we prototype the uncertain part? Is there production data that settles it? Debates about performance are particularly worth measuring rather than reasoning about, because intuition is unreliable there."

"If it genuinely can't be resolved, I'd look at which decision is easier to reverse — and take the reversible one. That reframes the argument from 'who's right' to 'what's the cheapest way to find out', which is a much more productive question."

"And I'd be genuinely open to being wrong. Changing my mind when shown a real problem is a positive signal, not a concession — I'd rather be corrected in review than in production, and someone who defends a position they can see is flawed is difficult to work with."

"If we still disagree and a decision is needed: disagree and commit. One of us decides — by ownership, or escalation if it's significant — and then we both implement it properly. Half-hearted implementation of a decision you lost is worse than either option, and it poisons the next disagreement."

"I'd also record the reasoning — a short decision record with the options and why we chose. Partly so it can be revisited when circumstances change, and partly so we don't relitigate it in six months having forgotten the context."

"One thing I'd watch: seniority isn't an argument. If I'm the more senior person, winning by position rather than reasoning costs me the next time someone has a better idea and doesn't raise it."

What they're checking
  • Do you look for the underlying disagreement?
  • Do you try to make it empirical?
  • Do you use reversibility as a tie-breaker?
  • Can you commit to a decision you disagreed with?
  • Do you avoid winning by seniority?
Key takeaways
  • These questions probe whether you've lived with a decision — answer with specifics, not principles.
  • "When would you not use X?" is the most reliable senior question. Have answers ready.
  • Microservices: independent deployability is the one benefit; a distributed monolith is the worst outcome.
  • A queue between two things that must be atomic replaces a transaction with a saga.
  • CQRS and event sourcing are different, and CQRS has cheap and expensive forms.
  • Prepare two "what I'd do differently" stories — one technical, one about process.
  • A regret needs defensible original reasoning and a specific quantified cost.
  • Prioritise debt by interest rate, and express it in outcomes the business feels.
  • Incident story order: impact, detection, diagnosis, mitigation, root cause, prevention.
  • Include a wrong turn — real diagnosis has one.
  • Prevention must be structural; a fix relying on memory isn't a fix.
  • Under deadline pressure, cut scope not quality — and protect what's expensive to change.
  • Have a line you won't cross: security and data integrity.
  • Find the real disagreement, prefer the reversible option, then disagree and commit.
