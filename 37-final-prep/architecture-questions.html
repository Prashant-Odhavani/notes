Architecture Questions

Questions with no single right answer. What's scored is whether you can name the trade-off and say what would change your mind.

🔴 Advanced ⭐ Senior-level
The answer shape that works for all of these

Every question here is best answered in four moves:

1. Ask what you'd need to know. Two or three specific questions, not "it depends".

2. Commit to a recommendation under stated assumptions. Refusing to choose reads as indecision.

3. Name the cost of your choice, before being asked.

4. Say what would change your mind — the threshold at which the other option wins.

That fourth move is the one candidates skip, and it's the strongest, because it demonstrates you know the boundary of your own decision rather than just its content.

Choosing a data store 🔥 Must know

SQL or NoSQL for a new system? 🔥 Must know
How I'd answer

"I'd ask three things: are the access patterns known and stable, does anything need multi-entity transactions, and how much do we need to query in ways we haven't anticipated?"

"My default is relational, and I'd defend that positively rather than as a fallback. It gives transactions across entities, flexible querying after the fact, constraints enforced by the database, and a very large pool of people who understand it. For most business systems that's exactly what you want."

"The property I'd emphasise is querying you didn't anticipate. Business requirements change, and a relational schema lets you answer a new question with a new query — possibly plus an index. A document store optimised for known access patterns makes an unanticipated query expensive or impossible."

"I'd choose a document store when the access patterns are genuinely known and stable, the data is naturally hierarchical and read as a unit, throughput or global distribution requirements exceed what a single relational primary gives, or the schema varies genuinely per item rather than accidentally."

"The cost I'd name: the partition key is usually irreversible. In Cosmos DB or DynamoDB you cannot change it without a migration, and it determines both the scale ceiling and which queries are cheap. So choosing it before you understand the access patterns and the size distribution is the expensive mistake — a skewed key gives you a hot partition, and adding capacity can't fix that because throughput divides per partition."

"I'd also push back on 'NoSQL means no schema'. There's still a schema — it just lives in the application and in every consumer, unversioned. That's usually harder to change, not easier."

"What would change my mind: a genuine need for single-digit-millisecond reads at very high volume, multi-region active-active writes, or a document shape that a relational model would fragment across eight tables and always join back together."

"And I'd say that using both is normal — relational as the system of record, a document store or search index as a projection for specific read patterns. That's usually better than forcing one store to do everything."

What they're checking
  • Do you have a default, and can you justify it positively?
  • Do you value unanticipated querying?
  • Do you know the partition key is irreversible?
  • Do you reject "no schema"?
  • Do you name a threshold that would change the answer?

Drawing boundaries 🔥 Must know

How do you decide where one service ends and another begins? 🔥 Must know
How I'd answer

"My primary test is what must be transactional together. If two things must change atomically, they belong in one service with one database. Splitting them means replacing a transaction with a saga — compensation logic, eventual consistency, partial-failure states — for no benefit."

"So I work outward from consistency requirements rather than from nouns in the requirements. 'We have orders, customers and products, so three services' produces boundaries that need distributed transactions to function."

"Second test: what changes together. If a typical feature touches three services, those boundaries are wrong — you've created coordination overhead without independence. That's measurable afterwards: look at how many services a typical pull request touches."

"Third: who owns it. A boundary that doesn't align with a team is a boundary nobody maintains. Conway's law applies whether you plan for it or not."

"The signal I'd use to judge an existing design: the number of sagas. Sagas are the cost of a boundary crossing something that should have been atomic, so lots of them means the boundaries are wrong."

"On data ownership: exactly one service owns each piece of data and everyone else reads via its API or a projection. Two services writing to the same table isn't microservices — it's coupling without independence, which is the worst of both. And I'd call out shared databases specifically, because they're the most common way a microservices migration goes wrong."

"What would change my mind about keeping something together: genuinely different scaling characteristics, a regulatory need to isolate a workload, or a component with a completely different release cadence owned by another team."

"And the honest default: start with fewer, larger boundaries. Merging two services is easy; splitting a wrongly-drawn one involves data migration. Given that you rarely know the right boundaries early, the asymmetry argues for starting coarse."

What they're checking
  • Is your primary test transactional rather than nominal?
  • Do you consider what changes together, and team ownership?
  • Do you use saga count as a signal?
  • Do you insist on single ownership of data?
  • Do you note the asymmetry between merging and splitting?

Consistency 🔥 Must know

Strong or eventual consistency? How do you decide? 🔥 Must know
How I'd answer

"I'd start by rejecting the framing slightly: it isn't a system-wide choice. Different parts of one system deserve different guarantees, and treating it as a single decision is the mistake."

"The question I'd actually ask per piece of data is 'what's the cost of being wrong?' A stale product description costs nothing. A stale stock level oversells. A stale permission check is a security incident. Those three obviously don't want the same guarantee."

"In a booking system, seat allocation must be strongly consistent — double-booking is unacceptable — while the search index can be seconds stale and nobody minds. Spending strong consistency only where it's needed is what makes the rest of the system scalable."

"Strong consistency costs: a single writer or coordination, which limits throughput and usually rules out multi-region active-active. Eventual consistency costs: every reader has to handle staleness, which shows up in the UI and in every consumer's logic."

"The specific staleness users notice is read-after-write. Placing an order and not seeing it in the list reads as a lost order, and produces a duplicate attempt. So I'd return the created resource in the response, or read a user's own recent writes from the authoritative store, or show the pending state honestly — 'your order is being processed' rather than an empty list."

"Most other staleness is invisible, which is why that one is worth designing around specifically."

"Two mechanisms I'd mention. Where strong consistency is needed, the tool is usually one atomic conditional write — a conditional UPDATE, a unique constraint, an ETag check — rather than a lock. And where eventual consistency is acceptable, an explicit Pending status makes the intermediate state safe to observe, which is what makes it tolerable to everything else reading the data."

"What would change my mind: if the business genuinely cannot tolerate any window — money movement, safety-critical allocation — then I'd accept the throughput cost. And in the other direction, if a strongly-consistent design was capping throughput below requirements, I'd look for the narrowest invariant that genuinely needs it rather than relaxing everything."

What they're checking
  • Do you reject it as a system-wide choice?
  • Do you frame it as the cost of being wrong?
  • Do you know read-after-write is the visible problem?
  • Do you reach for a conditional write rather than a lock?
  • Do you know a pending status makes intermediate state safe?

Synchronous or asynchronous 🔥 Must know

When should a service call be synchronous versus a message? 🔥 Must know
How I'd answer

"The test I'd use is: does the caller need the answer to continue?"

"If yes — a validation result, a price, an availability check — it's synchronous. Putting a queue there just adds a hop and a polling mechanism, so you've made it slower and given yourself two systems to debug."

"If no — send an email, update a search index, recalculate a report — it's a message. The caller returns immediately and the work happens reliably."

"The second question, which decides more than people expect: what should happen if the other side is down?"

"With a synchronous call, the caller fails — or worse, hangs. And slow is worse than dead: a dependency responding in 30 seconds instead of 200 milliseconds holds a thread per request, so the thread pool exhausts and every endpoint becomes slow, including ones that don't touch that dependency. One slow dependency takes down the whole service."

"With a message, the queue absorbs it. The work is delayed rather than lost, and the caller doesn't care. That's often the deciding argument — not latency, but failure containment."

"So if synchronous is unavoidable, it needs a timeout first — and HttpClient's 100-second default is far too long for a service call — then a circuit breaker so you stop paying the timeout on every request, then a bulkhead so that dependency can only consume a slice of your capacity."

"A middle option worth naming: accept the request synchronously, return 202 with a status URL, and process asynchronously. That gives the caller immediate acknowledgement with async durability, and it's the right shape for anything that takes longer than a request should."

"What would change my mind about going async: if the flow needs to be atomic with something else, a queue in the middle replaces a transaction with a saga — so I'd keep it synchronous and in one service. And if debuggability matters more than resilience for a low-risk internal call, synchronous is genuinely simpler: a stack trace beats correlating a message through a dead-letter queue."

What they're checking
  • Do you use "does the caller need the answer?" as the test?
  • Do you know slow is worse than dead?
  • Do you order timeout, breaker, bulkhead?
  • Do you know 202-with-status-URL as the middle option?
  • Do you note that a queue breaks a transaction?

Splitting a monolith ⭐ Senior-level

You've inherited a large monolith. How do you approach modernising it? ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"First: I wouldn't assume it needs splitting. 'Monolith' isn't a diagnosis. I'd want to know what's actually painful — is it deployment risk, build time, teams colliding, one component needing different scaling, or just that it's old and unloved? Those have different answers, and the last one isn't a problem."

"Then I'd stabilise before restructuring. Tests around the parts we'll touch, a reliable pipeline, and observability — because you cannot safely refactor something you can't deploy confidently or observe. That's unglamorous and it's the prerequisite; teams that skip it end up with a half-migrated system they can't reason about."

"Then modularise in place, before extracting anything. Establish module boundaries inside the codebase and enforce them mechanically — an architecture test that fails the build on cross-module references."

"That step gets undervalued and it delivers most of the benefit. If the pain is teams colliding, module boundaries solve it without any distributed-systems cost. And if you do extract later, the seams already exist and are already proven — so extraction becomes mechanical rather than archaeological."

"If extraction is genuinely justified, I'd use the strangler pattern: route traffic through a facade, move one capability at a time behind it, and keep the old path until the new one is proven. Never a big-bang rewrite — those have a poor record: they take longer than estimated, reintroduce fixed bugs, and stop feature work for months."

"What I'd extract first: something with clear boundaries, low coupling, and real value in being separate. Ideally something that's genuinely painful and genuinely separable. A first extraction that goes well buys credibility for the rest; one that turns into a six-month slog kills the programme."

"The hard part is always the data, and I'd say so explicitly. Splitting the code is comparatively easy; splitting a database where everything joins to everything is where these efforts die. The sequence is: identify the tables the extracted service owns, remove other components' direct access to them — replacing joins with API calls, which will be slower and is the real cost — and only then physically separate."

"Two failure modes I'd name. A shared database between the new service and the monolith — coupling without independence, all the cost and none of the benefit, and by far the most common outcome. And the endless migration: a system permanently half-migrated, running two architectures with double the operational surface, because the programme lost sponsorship. That's worse than either end state."

"So I'd want each step to deliver standalone value, so stopping partway still leaves things better rather than worse."

What they're checking
  • Do you interrogate whether splitting is needed at all?
  • Do you stabilise and observe before restructuring?
  • Do you modularise in place first?
  • Strangler rather than rewrite?
  • Do you identify the data as the hard part?
  • Do you name the shared-database and endless-migration failure modes?

Layering and structure 🟡 Intermediate

Clean architecture — worth it, or over-engineering? 🔴 Advanced
How I'd answer

"It depends on which part you mean, because it's usually adopted as a package and only some of it earns its keep."

"The valuable core is dependency inversion at the boundaries: domain logic that doesn't depend on EF Core, ASP.NET Core or a specific HTTP client. That makes the logic testable without infrastructure, and it means an infrastructure change doesn't ripple into business rules. Genuinely worth it."

"What often isn't worth it is the ceremony that comes with it. A repository interface with one implementation that wraps DbSet adds indirection while giving you nothing — DbSet is a repository and DbContext is a unit of work. Mapping between four representations of the same concept for every operation is real cost paid every time you touch it. And an interface per class, created reflexively, makes navigation harder for no benefit."

"So my position: keep the dependency direction, drop the ceremony. Domain logic free of infrastructure references, and otherwise the simplest structure that expresses the code."

"For a CRUD-heavy application I'd go further and say most of it is overhead. If an endpoint reads a row and returns it, routing that through a command, a handler, a repository, a domain entity and three mappers is pure cost — and it makes the codebase harder to learn."

"Where I'd invest the structure is where the logic is. Vertical slices work well here: organise by feature rather than by layer, so everything for one operation sits together, and apply more structure only to the slices that are genuinely complex. A simple lookup stays simple; a pricing calculation gets a proper domain model."

"What would change my mind toward more structure: a genuinely complex domain with real invariants and rules, a long-lived system where infrastructure will certainly be replaced, or a large team needing consistency more than local optimality. Those are real reasons — I'd just want one of them to be true."

"The general principle: structure should be proportional to the complexity it manages. Uniform structure applied regardless of complexity is a cost with no corresponding benefit."

What they're checking
  • Can you separate the valuable part from the ceremony?
  • Do you know DbSet is already a repository?
  • Do you vary structure with complexity rather than applying it uniformly?
  • Can you say what would justify more structure?
Key takeaways
  • Ask, recommend, name the cost, then say what would change your mind.
  • Relational by default — the value is querying you didn't anticipate.
  • Partition keys are irreversible; "no schema" means an unversioned schema in the application.
  • Draw boundaries from what must be transactional; saga count signals wrong boundaries.
  • One owner per piece of data — a shared database is coupling without independence.
  • Start coarse: merging is easy, splitting involves data migration.
  • Consistency is per-data, not system-wide — ask what being wrong costs.
  • Read-after-write is the staleness users actually notice.
  • Sync when the caller needs the answer; async for failure containment.
  • Slow is worse than dead — timeout, breaker, bulkhead, in that order.
  • 202 with a status URL is the underused middle option.
  • Modularise in place before extracting; strangler, never rewrite.
  • The data is the hard part of splitting a monolith.
  • Keep clean architecture's dependency direction, drop the ceremony.
  • Structure proportional to complexity, not uniform.
