Boundaries & Decomposition

The only decision that really matters. Get boundaries wrong and everything downstream is damage control.

🔴 Advanced ⭐ Senior-level 🔥 Must know
Quick revision
  • Split by business capability, never by technical layer or by entity.
  • A service owns its data. Nobody else reads its tables. That's the actual test.
  • A shared database means you don't have microservices — you have a distributed monolith.
  • Chatty services are a boundary error — the wrong seam was cut.
  • Bounded contexts from DDD are the best available guide.
  • The same word can mean different things in different contexts. That's a boundary signal, not a modelling failure.
  • Prefer too few, larger services. Merging is easy; splitting isn't.

The wrong ways to split 🔥 Must know

Split byWhy it fails
Technical layer — an API service, a business-logic service, a data service Every feature crosses every service. You've distributed the layers of a monolith, so nothing can be deployed independently
Entity — a Customer service, an Order service, a Product service Sounds right and usually isn't. Entities are used by capabilities, not owned by them. Leads to anaemic CRUD services that everything else calls constantly
Team convenience — one per developer Boundaries follow staffing rather than the domain, and change when people leave
Technology — everything Redis touches in one service Infrastructure isn't a domain concern
Size — "services should be small" Produces nanoservices. Ten services that must be called in sequence to do one thing
The entity-service trap is the most seductive one

"A Customer service, an Order service, a Product service" is the split almost every team reaches for first, because it maps to the database tables and feels tidy.

The problem is that entities aren't capabilities. Placing an order needs customer data, product data, pricing, inventory and payment — so the Order service calls four others synchronously for every request. That's five network hops, five failure modes, and a latency floor that's the sum of all of them.

And the services end up anaemic: a Customer service that only does CRUD isn't a business capability, it's a distributed data access layer. All the logic lives in whoever orchestrates it, so the boundaries deliver nothing.

The tell is a service whose API is Get, Create, Update, Delete and nothing else. That's a table with HTTP in front of it.

Split by business capability 🔥 Must know

In simple terms

A good service does one thing the business would recognise as a thing, and does all of it.

"Fulfilment" — picking, packing, shipping, tracking. "Billing" — invoicing, payment collection, dunning. Not "the Order table".

TestA good boundary
Can it be deployed alone?Yes, without coordinating other services
Does it own its data?Yes — nothing else reads its store directly
Can it complete its work locally?Mostly, without synchronous calls to others
Would a business person name it?Yes — "billing", "fulfilment", "search"
Does one team own it?Yes, end to end
Do changes stay inside it?Most feature work touches one service
Think of it this way

A restaurant isn't organised into a "plate department", a "food department" and a "customer department".

It's organised into kitchen, front of house and bar — each of which handles plates, food and customers as needed to do its own job completely.

Splitting by noun gives you departments that can't function alone. Splitting by capability gives you teams that can.

Bounded contexts ⭐ Senior-level

The idea that makes boundaries findable

A bounded context is a region where a term has one specific, consistent meaning.

The insight: the same word usually means different things in different parts of a business — and where the meaning changes is where the boundary is.

"Customer" in…Actually means
SalesA lead with a pipeline stage, an owner and a probability
BillingA payer with a payment method, a billing address and a credit limit
SupportA contact with a ticket history and an entitlement level
FulfilmentA delivery address and a set of delivery preferences
Senior signal — resist the single shared Customer model

The instinct is to build one canonical Customer that every service shares, because duplication looks wrong.

What happens is that the model accumulates every field any context needs — pipeline stage, credit limit, ticket history, delivery preferences — so it becomes enormous and nobody can change it, because a change affects every service. It becomes the coupling point the whole architecture was meant to avoid.

The correct answer is that each context has its own model of Customer, containing only what it needs, linked by a shared identifier. Billing's Customer has a credit limit; Fulfilment's has a delivery address. Neither knows about the other's fields.

That looks like duplication and isn't — they're different concepts that happen to share a name. Being able to make that argument is one of the clearest DDD signals, because the instinct to unify is strong and unifying is what creates the distributed monolith.

Practical ways to find the seams
  • Listen for language changes. When the same word means something different in another conversation, you've crossed a boundary.
  • Follow the org chart, cautiously. Conway's law means your architecture will resemble your communication structure anyway — so existing team boundaries are evidence, though not if the org is itself badly structured.
  • Look at change history. Files that always change together belong together. That's measurable from git rather than a matter of opinion.
  • Event storming. Map the domain events in time order; clusters of events with the same actors and data tend to be contexts.
  • Follow transaction boundaries. Things that must be atomic together should live together — this is the strongest technical constraint.

Data ownership is the real test 🔥 Must know

Remember this

Database-per-service is not an implementation detail. It's the definition.

If two services read the same tables, they're coupled at the schema — so neither can change its schema independently, and therefore neither can be deployed independently.

At which point you've paid for distribution and kept the coupling. The database is where microservices actually succeed or fail.

  Orders Service  ──┐
  Billing Service ──┼──►  [ One shared database ]
  Support Service ──┘

  - Adding a column risks breaking two other services
  - Nobody can migrate without coordinating with everyone
  - A slow query in Support degrades Orders
  - "Who is allowed to write to this table?" has no answer
  - You cannot deploy independently, which was the entire point
  Orders Service  ──►  [ Orders DB ]
  Billing Service ──►  [ Billing DB ]
  Support Service ──►  [ Support DB ]

  Data crosses boundaries by API call or by event -
  never by reading another service's tables.

The three hard shared-data problems

ProblemApproach
Service B needs some of A's data Replicate what it needs via events, keeping a local read-only copy. B queries its own store, so it works when A is down
A report spanning several services A dedicated reporting store fed by events from all of them. Do not join across service databases
Reference data everyone needs — countries, currencies Publish it and let each service cache a copy. It changes rarely, so staleness is harmless
Senior signal — data replication is the answer, and it needs to be defended

"Copy the data you need into your own store" sounds wrong to anyone with relational instincts — it's denormalisation across a network, with eventual consistency.

But the alternative is worse. If Billing calls Orders synchronously every time it needs order data, then Billing is down whenever Orders is down, and its latency includes Orders' latency. You've created a runtime dependency where you wanted an ownership boundary.

With replication, Billing holds the order fields it cares about, updated by events. It queries locally, so it's available independently — which is the fault isolation microservices are supposed to provide.

The costs to state honestly: the copy is eventually consistent, you need the outbox pattern so events aren't lost, and you need reconciliation because copies drift. Anyone proposing replication without a reconciliation plan hasn't run it in production.

This is the same reasoning as duplicating a document under two partition keys — pay on write to make reads independent.

How big should a service be? ⭐ Senior-level

More time in serialisation than in logicScaling means scaling things that don't need it
A change touches four repositoriesSlow test suite, slow pipeline
Err on the side of too few

The asymmetry matters: merging two services is straightforward; splitting one is hard.

Merging means moving code into one deployable and combining two schemas — unpleasant but bounded. Splitting means finding a boundary that may not exist, separating entangled data, introducing a saga where a transaction was, and doing it without downtime.

So when uncertain, keep it together. A service that turns out to be two is a manageable problem; two that should have been one is a permanent tax on every feature that spans them.

The useful heuristic is the transaction boundary: if two things must change atomically, they belong in the same service. Splitting across a transaction boundary is the decision that generates sagas, and sagas are the most expensive thing on the consistency page.

Key takeaways
  • Split by business capability. Never by layer, and be suspicious of splitting by entity.
  • A service whose API is only CRUD is a distributed data access layer, not a capability.
  • Bounded contexts: where a word's meaning changes is where the boundary is.
  • Resist a single shared model — each context keeps its own view, linked by identifier.
  • Data ownership is the definition, not a detail. A shared database means no independent deployment.
  • Replicate the data you need via events, so you stay available when the source is down.
  • Replication requires an outbox and a reconciliation plan.
  • Don't join across service databases — use a reporting store fed by events.
  • Prefer fewer, larger services. Merging is easy; splitting isn't.
  • Things that must be atomic together belong in the same service.

Common mistakes

  • Splitting by technical layer, so every feature crosses every service.
  • Entity services that only do CRUD.
  • A shared database across services.
  • Direct database reads into another service's tables.
  • One canonical shared model that nobody can change.
  • Synchronous calls for data that could be replicated.
  • Replication with no reconciliation job.
  • Joining across service databases for reporting.
  • Nanoservices requiring five hops per operation.
  • Splitting across a transaction boundary, forcing a saga.

Interview questions

Advanced / scenario

How would you decide where the service boundaries go in an e-commerce system? 🔥 Must know
How I'd answer

"I'd start by ruling out the two splits people usually reach for."

"Not by technical layer — an API service, a logic service, a data service. That distributes the layers of a monolith, so every feature crosses every service and nothing can be deployed independently."

"And I'd be very cautious about splitting by entity — a Customer service, an Order service, a Product service. It's the most seductive option because it maps to the tables, and it usually produces anaemic CRUD services. Placing an order then needs customer, product, pricing, inventory and payment data, so the Order service calls four others synchronously for every request: five hops, five failure modes, and a latency floor that's the sum of all of them. The tell is a service whose whole API is Get/Create/Update/Delete — that's a table with HTTP in front of it, and all the real logic ends up in whoever orchestrates them."

"What I'd split by is business capability — something the business would recognise as a thing, that does all of that thing. For e-commerce that's more like Catalogue, Basket, Ordering, Payments, Fulfilment, Identity. Fulfilment owns picking, packing, shipping and tracking — not 'the Shipment table'."

"To find those boundaries I'd use bounded contexts, and the practical technique is listening for where language changes meaning. 'Customer' in Sales is a lead with a pipeline stage. In Billing it's a payer with a credit limit. In Fulfilment it's a delivery address and preferences. Where the meaning shifts is where the boundary is."

"That leads to the point I'd emphasise: resist building one canonical Customer model. The instinct is to unify because duplication looks wrong. What happens is the model accumulates every field any context needs, becomes enormous, and then nobody can change it without affecting every service — so it becomes the exact coupling point the architecture was meant to remove. Each context should hold its own view of Customer with only the fields it needs, linked by a shared id. Those are different concepts sharing a name, not duplication."

"Then the test that actually decides whether a boundary is real: does the service own its data? Database-per-service isn't an implementation detail, it's the definition. If Orders and Billing read the same tables, neither can migrate its schema without coordinating — so neither can deploy independently, and that was the whole point."

"For data that legitimately crosses boundaries, I'd replicate rather than call synchronously. Billing keeps a local copy of the order fields it needs, updated by events, so it queries its own store and stays available when Ordering is down. That's the fault isolation you're supposedly buying. It's eventually consistent, it needs an outbox so events aren't lost, and it needs a reconciliation job because copies drift — I'd state all three rather than presenting it as free."

"For reporting across services, a separate reporting store fed by events. Never a join across service databases."

"Two practical constraints I'd apply. Follow transaction boundaries: if two things must change atomically, they belong in the same service — splitting across a transaction is what generates sagas, which are the most expensive thing here. And err on the side of fewer, larger services, because merging two is straightforward and splitting one is hard. A service that turns out to be two is manageable; two that should have been one taxes every feature that spans them."

"And honestly, for most e-commerce systems I'd start these as modules in one deployable with those boundaries enforced, and extract only where a specific capability proves it needs independent deployment."

What they're really checking
  • Do you reject layer-based splitting, and question entity-based?
  • Do you know bounded contexts and the language-change heuristic?
  • Will you argue against a canonical shared model?
  • Do you treat data ownership as the definitive test?
  • Do you prefer replication over synchronous calls, with its costs stated?
  • Do you respect transaction boundaries?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: a service per entity with no reflection.
  • Red flag: a shared database "for simplicity".
  • Red flag: one canonical model shared by all services.
Likely follow-ups
  • Basket and Ordering both need product prices. How?
  • How do you reconcile drifted replicas?
  • What if a report needs data from six services in real time?
