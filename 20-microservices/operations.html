Resilience & Observability

In a distributed system, partial failure is the normal state. These are the mechanisms that keep it from becoming total failure — and the tooling that lets you see it.

🔴 Advanced ⭐ Senior-level 🔥 Must know
Quick revision
  • A slow dependency is more dangerous than a dead one. Timeouts are mandatory.
  • Circuit breakers stop you hammering something that's already failing — and fail fast.
  • Bulkheads stop one dependency exhausting all your threads.
  • Only retry idempotent operations, with jitter.
  • Distributed tracing is not optional — a stack trace stops at the network boundary.
  • Liveness and readiness are different checks. Confusing them causes restart loops.
  • Test at the contract level, not by spinning up everything.

Timeouts, breakers and bulkheads 🔥 Must know

Why a slow service is worse than a dead one

A dead service fails immediately — you handle the error and move on.

A slow service holds your threads. Each waiting request occupies a thread; requests arrive faster than they complete; your thread pool exhausts; you now fail — including requests that had nothing to do with the slow dependency.

Then your callers do the same. That's a cascading failure, and it's how one struggling service takes down an entire system.

Timeout Waiting indefinitely Threads accumulate until the pool is exhausted
Retry Transient failures A momentary blip becomes a user-visible error
Circuit breaker Hammering a failing dependency You add load to something already struggling, and pay the timeout every request
Bulkhead One dependency consuming all capacity A slow non-critical call starves critical paths

builder.Services.AddHttpClient<PricingClient>(c =>
    {
        c.BaseAddress = new Uri("https://pricing");
        c.Timeout = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(10);          // overall ceiling
    })
    .AddStandardResilienceHandler(o =>
    {
        // Per-attempt timeout - must be shorter than the overall one
        o.AttemptTimeout.Timeout = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(3);

        o.Retry.MaxRetryAttempts = 3;
        o.Retry.BackoffType = DelayBackoffType.Exponential;
        o.Retry.UseJitter = true;                      // avoid synchronised retries

        // Open the circuit when 10% of calls in the window fail
        o.CircuitBreaker.FailureRatio = 0.1;
        o.CircuitBreaker.SamplingDuration = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(30);
        o.CircuitBreaker.BreakDuration = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(15);
    });
The timeout budget: retries multiply your latency ceiling

A 3-second per-attempt timeout with 3 retries is potentially 12 seconds before you give up — plus backoff delays. If the caller's own timeout is 5 seconds, your retries are pointless: the caller gave up long ago and you're doing work nobody will read.

Worse, in a chain each layer retrying multiplies. Three layers each retrying three times is up to 27 requests for one user action — so a struggling service at the bottom receives an order of magnitude more load exactly when it can least handle it. That's retry amplification.

The rule: each layer's total timeout must be shorter than its caller's, and retry at one level only. Usually the outermost, or the one closest to the failure. Deciding a timeout budget across the call chain — and writing it down — is the part teams skip.

Breaker stateBehaviour
ClosedRequests pass through; failures are counted
OpenFail immediately without calling. Gives the dependency room to recover
Half-openLet one request through. Success closes it; failure re-opens
Senior signal — the circuit breaker's real job is protecting the caller

People describe it as giving the failing service room to recover. That's a benefit and it's secondary.

The primary value is that you stop paying the timeout. With the circuit open you fail in microseconds instead of waiting 3 seconds — so your threads stay free and you stay healthy. That's what prevents the cascade.

Which leads to the question that actually matters and is usually unanswered: what do you do when the circuit is open? Failing fast is only useful if you have something to do with the fast failure.

Options: serve cached or stale data — often perfectly acceptable; return a degraded response with the optional part omitted; queue the work for later if it needn't be synchronous; or fail the request clearly with a 503 and Retry-After if the data is genuinely essential.

A breaker with no defined fallback just converts slow failures into fast ones. That's an improvement, and it isn't resilience.

Bulkheads: the detail people forget

Named after ship compartments — one flooded section doesn't sink the vessel.

Concretely: give each dependency its own limited concurrency allowance. If the recommendations service can only ever consume 10 concurrent slots, then even when it's slow, the other 90 remain available for checkout.

Without it, a slow non-critical dependency can consume every thread and take down your critical paths. That's a particularly frustrating outage: the important feature fails because an optional one was slow.

In Polly this is RateLimiter or a concurrency limiter in the pipeline. It's the least-used of these patterns and often the one that would have prevented the incident.

Distributed tracing 🔥 Must know

The problem it solves

In a monolith, an exception gives you a stack trace covering the whole operation.

Across services, the stack trace stops at the network boundary. You get "the call to Pricing failed" with no visibility into why. And the log lines for one user action are scattered across five services' logs with nothing linking them.

builder.Services.AddOpenTelemetry()
    .ConfigureResource(r => r.AddService("orders-api"))
    .WithTracing(t => t
        .AddAspNetCoreInstrumentation()        // incoming requests
        .AddHttpClientInstrumentation()        // outgoing calls - propagates context
        .AddEntityFrameworkCoreInstrumentation()
        .AddOtlpExporter())
    .WithMetrics(m => m
        .AddAspNetCoreInstrumentation()
        .AddRuntimeInstrumentation()
        .AddOtlpExporter());

// Logs correlate automatically - trace and span ids are attached
builder.Logging.AddOpenTelemetry(o => o.AddOtlpExporter());
ConceptMeaning
TraceOne end-to-end operation across all services
SpanOne unit of work within it — an HTTP call, a query
Trace IDShared by every span in the operation. The thing you search by
Context propagationPassing trace ids across boundaries via traceparent
BaggageCustom key-values carried along — tenant id, user id
Senior signal — the trace id must reach your logs and your users

Tracing tells you where time went. Logs tell you why. They're only useful together, and that requires the trace id to be on every log line — which AddOpenTelemetry on the logging pipeline does automatically, and which hand-rolled correlation usually gets wrong in async code.

The step most teams miss: return the trace id to the client on an error response.

Then a user reporting a problem can quote a reference, and you can retrieve the entire distributed trace for that exact request. Without it, you're searching by timestamp and hoping — and the traceId in a ProblemDetails response costs nothing to add.

One operational caveat: sampling. Tracing every request is expensive at volume, so you sample — but then the interesting request may not be sampled. Tail-based sampling (decide after seeing the whole trace, keeping errors and slow traces) is the answer, and it's worth knowing the trade-off exists rather than discovering the trace you need was discarded.

Health checks 🔥 Must know

builder.Services.AddHealthChecks()
    .AddDbContextCheck<AppDbContext>("database", tags: ["ready"])
    .AddRedis(redisConnection, "cache", tags: ["ready"])
    .AddUrlGroup(new Uri("https://pricing/health/live"), "pricing", tags: ["ready"]);

// LIVENESS: is the process alive? No dependency checks.
app.MapHealthChecks("/health/live", new() { Predicate = _ => false });

// READINESS: can it serve traffic? Dependencies included.
app.MapHealthChecks("/health/ready", new() { Predicate = c => c.Tags.Contains("ready") });
LivenessReadiness
QuestionIs the process alive?Can it serve traffic now?
Checks dependenciesNoYes
On failure the platformRestarts the containerRemoves it from load balancing
Should beTrivial and always fastMeaningful but cheap
Checking dependencies in liveness causes cluster-wide restart loops

This is the classic mistake. If liveness checks the database and the database has a brief blip, Kubernetes concludes every pod is unhealthy and restarts them all.

Restarting doesn't fix a database problem. And now you've lost all your warm caches and connection pools, so when the database recovers, every pod simultaneously reconnects and rebuilds — turning a 30-second blip into a multi-minute outage that you caused.

Liveness answers only "is this process wedged?" — a deadlock, a stuck event loop. Restart is the correct remedy for that and nothing else.

Readiness is where dependency checks belong: the pod stays running but stops receiving traffic, and recovers automatically when the dependency does.

A related trap: readiness that checks a downstream service can propagate unreadiness. If A reports unready because B is unready, and C checks A, then one failure marks the whole graph unready. Check the dependencies you genuinely cannot function without, and prefer a circuit breaker with a fallback over declaring yourself unready.

Deployment 🟡 Intermediate

StrategyHowTrade-off
RollingReplace instances graduallyDefault. Both versions run at once — so contracts must be compatible
Blue-greenTwo environments; switch trafficInstant rollback; double the infrastructure
CanarySmall traffic share to the new versionBest risk reduction; needs good metrics to judge
Feature flagsDeploy dark, enable separatelyDecouples deploy from release; flags accumulate
Rolling deployment means both versions run simultaneously

During the rollout, v1 and v2 of a service are both live and both handling traffic. So every change must be backward and forward compatible for the duration.

That constrains more than API shapes. A database migration must work with both versions of the code — so you cannot rename a column in one step. The pattern is expand/contract: add the new column, write to both, migrate readers, then remove the old one across separate releases.

Same for messages: v2 might publish an event v1 consumers don't understand. Additive changes only, and consumers must tolerate unknown fields.

This is the same discipline as API versioning, applied to schemas and message formats.

Feature flags separate deployment from release

The most useful property: you can deploy code at 2pm on a Tuesday with the flag off, then turn it on when you're ready — and turn it off instantly if it misbehaves, without a deployment.

That converts "roll back a release" into "flip a switch", which is dramatically faster and lower risk during an incident.

The cost is real and worth stating: flags accumulate. Every flag doubles the paths through the code, and a codebase with forty stale flags is untestable in any meaningful sense. So each flag needs an owner and a removal date, and removing them has to be actual work someone does.

Testing across services ⭐ Senior-level

LevelTestsProportion
UnitDomain logic in isolationMost
Service / integrationOne service with real database, stubbed dependenciesMany
ContractConsumer expectations verified against the producerOne per interaction — critical
End-to-endEverything running togetherVery few
Senior signal — end-to-end tests don't scale with service count

The instinct is to test the whole system together. With eight services that means an environment running all eight plus their databases and brokers, kept consistent, for every pipeline run.

What happens: the suite becomes slow (tens of minutes), flaky (any of eight services can be misbehaving), and ambiguous — a failure doesn't tell you which service is at fault. Teams then start re-running until green, at which point the suite has negative value: it consumes CI time and trains people to ignore failures.

It also reintroduces coordination — you need a shared environment with compatible versions of everything, which is exactly what independent deployability was meant to eliminate.

Contract tests are the answer. Each consumer declares what it needs; the producer's pipeline verifies it still provides that, without running the consumer. So breakage is caught in the producer's own build, in seconds, with an unambiguous cause.

Then keep a handful of end-to-end tests for genuinely critical journeys — place an order, complete a payment — and accept they're slow. Being able to argue for contract tests over a large E2E suite is one of the better testing signals for a distributed system.

Testcontainers for the integration layer

Service-level tests need a real database — an in-memory provider doesn't catch SQL translation problems, constraint violations or migration errors.

Testcontainers starts a real SQL Server, Postgres or Redis in Docker for the test run, so you get genuine behaviour with no shared environment to maintain and no cleanup between runs.

That's the sweet spot for most tests: real infrastructure, stubbed services. It catches the class of bug an in-memory provider hides while remaining fast enough to run in CI on every commit.

Key takeaways
  • A slow dependency exhausts your threads and cascades. Timeouts are mandatory, not optional.
  • Each layer's total timeout must be shorter than its caller's; retry at one level only.
  • A circuit breaker's main value is that you stop paying the timeout.
  • A breaker without a defined fallback just makes failures faster.
  • Bulkheads stop a non-critical dependency starving critical paths.
  • Distributed tracing is required — a stack trace ends at the network boundary.
  • Put the trace id on every log line and return it to the client on errors.
  • Liveness must not check dependencies, or a blip becomes a cluster-wide restart loop.
  • Rolling deployment means both versions run at once — including against the database.
  • Feature flags turn rollback into a switch, and they accumulate.
  • Contract tests scale; large end-to-end suites don't.

Common mistakes

  • No timeout on outbound calls.
  • Retries at every layer, multiplying load on a failing service.
  • Retrying non-idempotent operations.
  • A circuit breaker with no fallback behaviour.
  • No bulkhead, so an optional dependency takes down critical paths.
  • Dependency checks in liveness, causing restart loops.
  • Readiness that propagates unreadiness across the service graph.
  • No distributed tracing, debugging by correlating timestamps.
  • Trace id not returned to clients.
  • Renaming a database column in one release during a rolling deploy.
  • A large end-to-end suite instead of contract tests.
  • Stale feature flags nobody removes.

Interview questions

Advanced / scenario

One service slows down and the whole system becomes unavailable. What happened, and how do you prevent it? 🔥 Must know
How I'd answer

"That's a cascading failure, and the key insight is that a slow dependency is more dangerous than a dead one."

"A dead service fails immediately — you get a connection refused, handle the error, and move on. A slow service holds your threads. Each waiting request occupies one, requests arrive faster than they complete, and your thread pool exhausts. Now you fail — including requests that never touched the slow dependency. Then your callers do the same thing, and it propagates upward."

"So the mechanism is thread exhaustion, not the dependency's failure. That's why 'the pricing service got slow' can produce 'the entire site is down'."

"Preventing it, in order of importance:"

"Timeouts on every outbound call. This is the non-negotiable one — without it you wait indefinitely and the rest doesn't matter. And the timeout has to be shorter than your caller's, or you're doing work nobody will read."

"A circuit breaker. After a failure threshold it opens and fails immediately without calling. People describe this as giving the failing service room to recover — that's secondary. The primary benefit is that you stop paying the timeout: you fail in microseconds instead of three seconds, so your threads stay free and you stay healthy."

"But the question that follows is the one usually unanswered: what do you do when the circuit is open? A breaker with no fallback just converts slow failures into fast ones. Options are serving cached or stale data — often perfectly acceptable — returning a degraded response with the optional part omitted, queueing the work if it needn't be synchronous, or failing clearly with a 503 and Retry-After if the data is genuinely essential. That decision is per-dependency and it's part of the design."

"A bulkhead, which is the one people forget. Give each dependency its own limited concurrency allowance — if recommendations can only ever consume 10 slots, then even when it's slow the other 90 remain available for checkout. Without it, a slow optional feature can starve your critical paths, which is a particularly annoying outage."

"And I'd check the retry configuration, because retries often make this worse. Three layers each retrying three times is up to 27 requests for one user action — so a struggling service receives an order of magnitude more load precisely when it can least handle it. Retry at one level only, with jitter so clients don't synchronise."

"In .NET all of this is AddStandardResilienceHandler on a typed HttpClient, which gives timeout, retry with jitter, and a circuit breaker with sensible defaults — so there's little excuse for not having it."

"The structural point I'd make: if a call being slow can take me down, I should question whether it needs to be synchronous at all. If the data could be replicated via events and read locally, or the work could be queued, the failure mode disappears rather than being mitigated. Resilience patterns manage a coupling; removing the coupling is better where it's possible."

What they're really checking
  • Do you know slow is worse than dead, and why — thread exhaustion?
  • Do you know the breaker protects the caller?
  • Do you have a fallback strategy rather than just failing fast?
  • Do you know about bulkheads?
  • Do you spot retry amplification?
  • Do you question the synchronous coupling itself?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: "add retries" — which makes it worse.
  • Red flag: a circuit breaker with no fallback.
  • Red flag: no mention of timeouts.
Likely follow-ups
  • How do you choose the timeout value?
  • What do you serve when the circuit is open?
  • How would you have detected this before users did?
How do you debug a request that failed somewhere across eight services? ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"This is the question that makes observability non-optional rather than nice to have. In a monolith an exception gives me a stack trace covering the whole operation. Across services, the stack trace stops at the network boundary — I get 'the call to Pricing failed' and nothing about why."

"So the foundation is distributed tracing. Every request gets a trace id at the edge, propagated to every downstream call via the traceparent header. Each service records spans — an incoming request, a database query, an outbound call — all sharing that trace id."

"Then I can pull up one trace and see the whole operation as a waterfall: which service was slow, which one errored, how long each hop took, and what the call graph actually was. In .NET that's OpenTelemetry with the ASP.NET Core, HttpClient and EF Core instrumentation, and it's mostly configuration rather than code."

"Tracing tells me where. Logs tell me why. So the trace id has to be on every log line — which AddOpenTelemetry on the logging pipeline does automatically, and which hand-rolled correlation usually gets wrong in async code. Then I search logs by trace id and get every line from every service for that one request, in order."

"The step most teams miss: return the trace id to the client on errors. Put it in the ProblemDetails response. Then a user reporting a problem quotes a reference and I retrieve the exact trace — instead of searching by timestamp and hoping. It costs nothing and it's the difference between minutes and hours."

"Metrics for the pattern rather than the instance — request rate, error rate and latency percentiles per service. That's what tells me whether this is one bad request or a systemic problem, and it's what I'd alert on. Traces explain individual failures; metrics tell me there's something to investigate."

"Two practical caveats I'd raise."

"Sampling. Tracing every request at volume is expensive, so you sample — and then the interesting request may not have been sampled, which is infuriating. Tail-based sampling is the answer: decide after seeing the whole trace, and always keep errors and slow traces. Worth knowing the trade-off exists rather than discovering the trace you need was discarded."

"Asynchronous flows. Trace context doesn't propagate through a message broker automatically — you have to put traceparent in the message headers and restore it in the consumer. Otherwise the trace stops at the publish and the consumer's work looks unrelated. Most .NET messaging libraries handle this now, but it's worth verifying rather than assuming, because a broken trace across a queue is exactly where the hard bugs live."

"And I'd add baggage for domain context — tenant id, user id — carried across the whole trace. Then I can answer 'is this affecting one tenant or everyone?', which is usually the first question during an incident."

What they're really checking
  • Do you know a stack trace doesn't cross service boundaries?
  • Do you know traces and logs must be correlated by trace id?
  • Do you return the trace id to clients?
  • Do you distinguish metrics from traces?
  • Do you know sampling and async propagation are gaps?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: "check the logs in each service".
  • Red flag: correlating by timestamp.
  • Red flag: no mention of context propagation.
Likely follow-ups
  • How does trace context survive a message queue?
  • What would you alert on?
  • How do you keep tracing costs reasonable?
