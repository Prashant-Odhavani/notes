Storage Accounts

Cheap, durable and easy to misconfigure. The interesting decisions are redundancy, tiers and how you hand out access.

🟢 Beginner 🟡 Intermediate 🔥 Must know
Quick revision
  • Blob for files, Queue for simple messaging, Table for cheap key-value.
  • Tiers trade storage cost against read cost. Archive needs rehydration.
  • Redundancy: LRS → ZRS → GRS. Zone, then region.
  • Geo-redundant replication is asynchronous — failover can lose recent writes.
  • SAS tokens for direct client access, so files never pass through your API.
  • User delegation SAS over account key SAS — revocable, and no key involved.
  • Lifecycle rules move and delete data automatically; that's your cost control.
  • ETags give you optimistic concurrency on blobs.

Storage Queues versus Service Bus

Storage Queues are much cheaper and genuinely fine for simple work distribution — a background job list, thumbnail generation, deferred emails.

What you don't get: topics and subscriptions, sessions for ordering, duplicate detection, transactions, and a real dead-letter queue. Messages are 64 KB, and there's no publish-subscribe at all.

Poison-message handling is manual: you read DequeueCount yourself and move the message somewhere after N attempts. Service Bus does that for you.

The reasonable rule: Storage Queues until you need a Service Bus feature, and be honest that you probably will. Migrating later is a code change in producers and consumers, so if the design already implies pub/sub or ordering, start with Service Bus.

The one thing Storage Queues do better: a very large backlog, up to the account limit. If you might accumulate tens of millions of messages, that's a real advantage.

Two traps in the tier table

Archive is not readable. You cannot read an archived blob at all — you must rehydrate it to Hot or Cool first, which takes up to 15 hours at standard priority (high priority is typically under an hour, and costs more).

So Archive is for data you're keeping for compliance, not data you might need on a Tuesday. If a user could plausibly request it and expect a response, Archive is the wrong tier.

Early deletion charges. Each tier has a minimum retention — 30, 90 and 180 days. Delete or move a blob before that and you're billed as though it stayed for the full period.

Which means a lifecycle rule that moves data to Archive after 30 days and deletes it after 60 costs more than leaving it in Cool, because you pay the full 180 days of Archive anyway plus the transition operations. Genuinely counterintuitive, and a real source of unexpected bills.

Model it before assuming a colder tier is cheaper. Cheaper storage plus expensive access can total more than expensive storage plus cheap access — it depends entirely on the read pattern.

Senior signal — snapshots and versions are the hidden cost

If versioning or soft delete is enabled, every overwrite creates a retained version — and those versions are billed like any other blob.

A file overwritten hourly for a year is 8,760 stored copies. Nobody notices until the storage bill is inexplicably large relative to the visible data, and the portal's blob list doesn't show versions by default, so the data appears to be a fraction of what you're paying for.

So lifecycle rules must cover snapshots and versions explicitly, not just base blobs. That's the part usually left out, and it's the part that accumulates.

Versioning and soft delete are still worth having — they're your protection against accidental deletion and ransomware. Just bound their retention deliberately rather than leaving it unlimited.

Geo-replication is asynchronous, so failover can lose data

This is the detail that matters and the one most often missed. Writes are committed locally and replicated to the paired region afterwards.

So there's a replication lag, and if the primary region is lost, anything not yet replicated is gone. The metric is Last Sync Time, and it's the honest measure of your recovery point objective — usually minutes, but not zero and not guaranteed.

Two consequences worth stating:

  • GRS is not a zero-data-loss guarantee. If your requirement is zero loss, storage redundancy alone doesn't provide it.
  • Without the RA- prefix, the secondary is invisible. You cannot read it, and the data is only accessible after a failover — which historically was Microsoft-initiated, though customer-managed failover is now available. Teams have assumed GRS meant they could read from the second region; it doesn't.

And note the direction of the tiering choice: ZRS protects against the more likely failure. A zone outage is far more common than losing an entire region, and ZRS is synchronous — no data loss. So ZRS or GZRS is usually the better default than plain GRS.

Redundancy is not backup

Every redundancy option replicates faithfully. If you delete a blob, or encrypt it with ransomware, or a bug overwrites it, that change is replicated to every copy.

Redundancy protects against infrastructure failure. It does nothing about mistakes or malice.

For those you need soft delete (a recovery window for deleted blobs and containers), versioning (previous contents of overwritten blobs), and for regulated or ransomware-sensitive data, immutability policies — a time-based legal hold that even an account owner cannot delete within the retention window.

That last one is the genuine ransomware defence, because it's the only option an attacker with full credentials can't undo. Worth knowing as the answer to "how do you protect backups from a compromised admin?"

// The client uploads straight to storage. The file never passes through your API.
public async Task<Uri> CreateUploadUrlAsync(string blobName, CancellationToken ct)
{
    // A user delegation key is signed by Entra ID - no account key involved
    var delegationKey = await _blobService.GetUserDelegationKeyAsync(
        startsOn: DateTimeOffset.UtcNow.AddMinutes(-5),   // clock skew allowance
        expiresOn: DateTimeOffset.UtcNow.AddMinutes(15),  // short: it only needs to start an upload
        cancellationToken: ct);

    var sas = new BlobSasBuilder
    {
        BlobContainerName = "uploads",
        BlobName          = blobName,
        Resource          = "b",
        ExpiresOn         = DateTimeOffset.UtcNow.AddMinutes(15),
        // Write only. No read, no delete, no list.
        Protocol          = SasProtocol.Https,
    };
    sas.SetPermissions(BlobSasPermissions.Write);

    // Optional but strong: restrict to the caller's IP range
    // sas.IPRange = new SasIPRange(callerIp, callerIp);

    var uri = new UriBuilder(_blobService.Uri)
    {
        Path  = $"uploads/{blobName}",
        Query = sas.ToSasQueryParameters(delegationKey, _blobService.AccountName).ToString()
    };
    return uri.Uri;
}
Senior signal — user delegation SAS, and why revocability is the point

A SAS signed with the account key has a problem that isn't obvious until you need to fix it: you cannot revoke it. Once issued, it's valid until it expires. If a token leaks — logged by a proxy, pasted into a support ticket, sitting in browser history — the only remedy is rotating the account key, which invalidates every other SAS and breaks anything using that key.

A user delegation SAS is signed by an Entra-issued delegation key instead. That makes it revocable — revoke the delegation key and every SAS derived from it stops working immediately — and it means no account key is involved at all, so you can disable key access on the account entirely.

It also carries the identity of the principal that created it, so storage logs show who issued the access rather than "someone with the key."

Beyond that, the discipline: short expiry, narrowest permission, HTTPS only, and an IP range where you know it. A write-only SAS valid for 15 minutes is a very different risk from a read-write SAS valid for a year — and the year-long one is what you find in codebases.

Also worth saying: a SAS in a URL is in logs. Proxies, CDNs, browser history and referrer headers all capture query strings, which is another argument for short expiry.

Why direct upload matters architecturally

Routing file uploads through your API means the file consumes your bandwidth, your memory, your request timeout and your instance count. A 2 GB upload occupies a web instance for its whole duration, and concurrent large uploads will exhaust the app before storage notices anything.

With a SAS, the client uploads directly to storage, which is built for exactly that and scales independently. Your API only issues a short-lived URL — a fast, cheap call.

The same pattern in reverse for downloads: issue a read SAS rather than streaming bytes through the API.

The follow-up you should be ready for: how do you know the upload happened, and validate it? The answer is Event Grid — subscribe to BlobCreated and validate, scan or process there. The client never tells you it finished; storage does.

Concurrency and consistency 🟡 Intermediate

// Read, including the ETag
var response = await blob.DownloadContentAsync(cancellationToken: ct);
var etag = response.Value.Details.ETag;

var updated = Modify(response.Value.Content);

try
{
    // Write only if nobody else changed it since we read
    await blob.UploadAsync(updated, new BlobUploadOptions
    {
        Conditions = new BlobRequestConditions { IfMatch = etag }
    }, ct);
}
catch (RequestFailedException ex) when (ex.Status == 412)
{
    // Precondition failed - someone else wrote first. Re-read and retry.
}

// Create-only, never overwrite: the atomic "claim this name" primitive
await blob.UploadAsync(content, new BlobUploadOptions
{
    Conditions = new BlobRequestConditions { IfNoneMatch = ETag.All }
}, ct);   // 409 if it already exists
Senior signal — the same conditional-write primitive as every other store

IfMatch with an ETag is optimistic concurrency: write only if unchanged. IfNoneMatch = ETag.All is write only if absent — an atomic claim.

That second one is the useful primitive, because it gives you a distributed lock or a leader election with no extra service: whoever creates the blob wins, everyone else gets 409. Blob leases build on the same idea with an expiry, which is how the Event Hubs processor and many Functions singletons coordinate.

And it's the same shape you've already seen: DynamoDB's condition expressions, Cosmos DB's _etag, Redis's SET NX, SQL's rowversion. Every store gives you one atomic conditional write, and every correct distributed coordination pattern is built on it.

Recognising that as one idea rather than five APIs is what the question is usually testing. And the corollary is just as important: read-then-write without a condition is a race, in every one of these stores.

Blob operations are strongly consistent; listing is not the same thing

A successful write is immediately visible to a subsequent read of that blob. Blob storage is strongly consistent per blob, which is worth knowing because people assume eventual consistency by default in cloud storage — that was S3's historical behaviour, not Azure's.

What's not immediate is listing and metadata-driven views: the blob index, and Data Lake directory semantics, can lag slightly.

So "write then read the blob" is safe. "Write then immediately list the container and expect to see it" is less reliable, and a workflow built on listing to discover new files is both slower and racier than reacting to an Event Grid notification.

The per-blob limit is the one that surprises people

Account-level throughput is generous, so teams reason "we're nowhere near the limit" and still get throttled.

The reason is that limits also apply per blob and per partition, at a much lower level. A single configuration file read by 5,000 instances on startup, or one blob every client polls, will throttle while the account is almost idle.

Same shape as a hot partition everywhere else: the aggregate limit isn't the one you hit.

Fixes, in order: cache it (a config blob should be read once, not per request), put a CDN in front of anything widely read, or spread the reads across copies if it genuinely must be that hot.

And the naming detail: blob names are partitioned by prefix, so sequential names like a timestamp prefix concentrate writes on one partition range. A high-ingest workload wants a high-cardinality prefix — hash first, then the readable part — which is the same advice as for any range-partitioned store.

Key takeaways
  • Blob for files, Queue for cheap simple messaging, Table for cheap key-value.
  • Storage Queues lack topics, sessions, transactions and a real DLQ.
  • Archive isn't readable — rehydration takes hours.
  • Minimum retention periods mean a colder tier can cost more.
  • Lifecycle rules must cover snapshots and versions, or they accumulate silently.
  • ZRS protects against the more likely failure and is synchronous.
  • Geo-replication is asynchronous — failover can lose recent writes.
  • Without RA-, you can't read the secondary.
  • Redundancy is not backup: soft delete, versioning, immutability.
  • Managed identity for your services; user delegation SAS for clients.
  • Account-key SAS cannot be revoked without rotating the key.
  • Direct upload via SAS keeps large files out of your API.
  • ETag conditions give optimistic concurrency and an atomic claim.
  • Per-blob limits throttle long before account limits.

Common mistakes

  • Uploading files through the API instead of a SAS direct to storage.
  • Long-lived account-key SAS tokens that can't be revoked.
  • Account keys in configuration instead of managed identity.
  • A read-write SAS where write-only was needed.
  • Archiving data that might be requested, then discovering rehydration takes hours.
  • Lifecycle rules that ignore versions and snapshots.
  • Assuming GRS means zero data loss, or that the secondary is readable.
  • Treating redundancy as backup.
  • Read-then-write with no ETag condition.
  • One hot blob, throttled while the account is idle.
  • Sequential blob name prefixes on a high-ingest workload.
  • Listing a container to find new files instead of reacting to events.

Interview questions

Intermediate

Users need to upload large files. Design it. 🔥 Must know
How I'd answer

"The key decision is that the file must not pass through my API. The client uploads directly to blob storage using a short-lived SAS URL that the API issues."

"The reason is resource consumption. Routing a 2 GB upload through the API means it occupies a web instance for the entire duration — bandwidth, memory, request timeout, a thread. A handful of concurrent large uploads exhausts the app while storage sits idle. And you'd be scaling web instances to handle byte shuffling, which is the wrong thing to scale."

"With direct upload, the API call is just 'give me a URL' — fast and cheap — and storage handles the bytes, which is what it's built for."

"The flow is: client asks for an upload URL, API authorises the request and decides the blob name, API returns a user delegation SAS with write-only permission and a short expiry, client uploads directly. For very large files the SDK does chunked block upload with retries and resume, which I'd get for free rather than implement."

"On the SAS specifically, I'd use a user delegation SAS rather than one signed with the account key. Two reasons. It's revocable — an account-key SAS is valid until it expires and the only way to kill it is rotating the key, which breaks everything else using that key. And it involves no account key at all, so key access can be disabled on the account entirely."

"Scoping: write permission only, no read, no delete, no list. Expiry in minutes, not days — it only needs to start an upload. HTTPS only. IP-restricted if I know the caller's range. And the API chooses the blob name, never the client, so a client can't overwrite someone else's file or escape the container with a path."

"Then the important part: how do I know the upload finished? The client telling me isn't reliable — it can crash, or lie. So I'd use Event Grid on the storage account, subscribing to BlobCreated, which triggers a function to validate and process."

"That's where I'd do content-type verification, virus scanning, thumbnailing, and updating the database record to mark the upload complete. It's authoritative because storage is reporting it, not the client."

"I'd note that Event Grid is at-least-once and unordered, so that handler needs to be idempotent — using the event id — and I'd configure a dead-letter container, since dead-lettering is off by default and failed events are otherwise silently discarded."

"A couple of things I'd add for completeness. Uploads land in a quarantine container and only move to the real one after validation, so unvalidated content is never servable. A lifecycle rule deletes abandoned uploads after a few days, because clients get URLs and never use them and those blobs accumulate. And downloads work the same way in reverse — a read SAS rather than streaming through the API, with a CDN in front if the content is widely read."

What they're really checking
  • Do you keep large files out of the application tier?
  • Do you know user delegation SAS and why revocability matters?
  • Do you scope permission and expiry tightly?
  • Do you use Event Grid rather than trusting the client's completion signal?
  • Do you handle abandoned uploads and quarantine?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: posting the file to an API endpoint.
  • Red flag: a long-lived read-write SAS.
  • Red flag: trusting the client to confirm completion.
Likely follow-ups
  • How do you stop a client uploading to another user's path?
  • How do you scan for malware before the file is servable?
  • What if the upload fails halfway?

Advanced

Which redundancy option would you choose, and what does it not protect you from? ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"My default would be ZRS, or GZRS if the data justifies regional protection — and I'd explain why in terms of which failures are actually likely."

"LRS keeps three copies in one datacentre. It survives disk and rack failure, which is the common hardware case, and it's the cheapest. Fine for data you can regenerate — caches, intermediate processing output, transient files."

"ZRS spreads copies across availability zones, so it survives an entire zone going down. That's the failure that actually happens — a datacentre losing power or cooling — far more often than a whole region being lost. And crucially it's synchronous, so there's no data loss when a zone fails."

"So the reasoning I'd give: ZRS protects against the more probable failure, with no recovery-point penalty, for a modest premium. That's a better default than jumping to geo-redundancy."

"GRS and GZRS add the paired region. Worth it for data you can't recreate — customer documents, financial records, backups. And here's the part I'd make sure to state: geo-replication is asynchronous."

"So writes commit locally and replicate afterwards. If the primary region is lost, anything not yet replicated is gone. The Last Sync Time metric is the honest measure of the recovery point — usually minutes, but not zero and not contractually guaranteed. GRS is not a zero-data-loss solution, and if that's the requirement, storage redundancy alone doesn't deliver it."

"I'd also flag that plain GRS doesn't let you read the secondary. That needs the RA- variants. Teams have assumed GRS meant they could serve reads from a second region and discovered otherwise during a drill. And even with RA-GRS, the secondary is read-only and lagging, so it's suitable for reporting or degraded read-only operation — not a hot standby you can write to."

"Now the more important half of the question — what redundancy doesn't protect against."

"Redundancy is not backup. Every option replicates faithfully. If someone deletes a container, or ransomware encrypts your blobs, or a deployment bug overwrites files, that change is replicated to all six copies, cheerfully and immediately. Redundancy addresses infrastructure failure and nothing else."

"So for the failures that are actually more likely — human error and malice — I'd want three separate things:"

"Soft delete for blobs and containers, giving a recovery window after deletion. Container-level soft delete matters as much as blob-level, because deleting a container is the more destructive mistake."

"Versioning, so an overwrite doesn't destroy the previous content. With retention bounded by a lifecycle rule, because otherwise versions accumulate and you're paying for thousands of copies of a frequently-overwritten file — that's a real and commonly missed cost."

"Immutability policies for anything regulated or ransomware-sensitive — a time-based retention policy that even an account owner cannot delete within the window. That's the only one an attacker with full credentials can't undo, so it's the genuine ransomware answer, and it's what I'd point to for 'how do you protect backups from a compromised administrator?'"

"And I'd separate account-level protection from cross-account protection. If the whole subscription is compromised, everything in it is at risk. For genuinely critical data, a copy in a different subscription with different credentials — or object replication to a locked-down account — is the real isolation. Redundancy within one account never gives you that."

What they're really checking
  • Do you reason about which failures are likely, not just tiers?
  • Do you know geo-replication is asynchronous?
  • Do you know the secondary isn't readable without RA-?
  • Do you separate redundancy from backup?
  • Do you know immutability is the ransomware answer?
  • Do you raise cross-account isolation?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: "GRS, because more copies is better."
  • Red flag: treating geo-redundancy as backup.
  • Red flag: assuming failover is lossless.
Likely follow-ups
  • How would you test a regional failover?
  • What's your RPO with GRS, and how do you measure it?
  • How do you stop versioning inflating the bill?
