Event-Driven Architecture

Components react to things that happened instead of being told what to do. Powerful, and more expensive than it first looks.

🟡 Intermediate 🔴 Advanced ⭐ Senior-level
Quick revision
  • Command = "do this", one handler, can be rejected. Event = "this happened", any number of listeners, can't be rejected.
  • Events are named in the past tense. If it reads like an instruction, it's a command wearing an event's name.
  • Choreography = each service reacts independently. Orchestration = one coordinator drives the steps.
  • Event-driven buys you decoupling and independent evolution. It costs you traceability, ordering and consistency.
  • The hardest part isn't publishing — it's knowing what's happening when a flow spans six services and nobody owns the whole thing.
  • Start with events at genuine boundaries. Not between two classes in the same process.

Commands vs events 🔥 Must know

Remember this

A command tells one specific handler to do something. The sender knows who, and expects it might be rejected.

An event announces that something already happened. The publisher doesn't know who's listening and can't be told "no".

Common mistake — the command dressed as an event

Publishing SendWelcomeEmail to a topic. That's a command with an event's transport. The giveaway: it's imperative, exactly one thing should handle it, and the publisher clearly does know what should happen next.

The event version is UserRegistered. Then the email service decides to send a welcome email — and later, analytics can subscribe too, without the publisher changing.

How to choose: if consumers need a lot of data or must keep working when the source is down, carry the state. If the data is large, changes fast, or is sensitive, send a notification and let consumers fetch what they need.

A common middle ground is to include the handful of fields most consumers need, plus the ID for anyone who needs more.

Senior signal

Pointing out that fat events create a schema contract. Once six services read CustomerEmail from your event, you can't remove that field without coordinating six deployments. Thin events keep the contract small but push availability coupling back onto the source.

There's no free option — and saying that is the answer.

Choreography vs orchestration 🔥 Must know

Take an order flow: payment → inventory → shipping → notification. Two ways to run it.

Choreography — everyone reacts

Order service    ── publishes ──▶  OrderPlaced
                                        │
        ┌───────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                               ▼                               ▼
  Payment service              Inventory service              Analytics service
  (reserves funds)             (reserves stock)               (records the event)
        │                               │
        ▼                               ▼
  PaymentAuthorized              StockReserved
        │                               │
        └───────────┬───────────────────┘
                    ▼
             Shipping service  ──▶ OrderShipped ──▶ Notification service

Good: no central bottleneck. Adding a consumer requires no change to anyone else. Services are genuinely decoupled.

Bad: the overall process exists nowhere. No file describes the flow. Debugging "why did order 123 never ship?" means checking six services' logs. And changing the sequence means changing several services.

Orchestration — one coordinator

                    ┌──────────────────────────┐
                    │  Order Orchestrator      │   ← the process lives HERE
                    │  (saga / workflow)       │
                    └───┬──────┬──────┬────────┘
         1. AuthorizePayment  │      │
              ▼               │      │
        Payment service       │      │
                    2. ReserveStock  │
                              ▼      │
                       Inventory service
                                 3. ArrangeShipping
                                     ▼
                               Shipping service

Good: the whole process is readable in one place. Easy to see where an order got stuck. Compensation logic (refund the payment if shipping fails) has an obvious home.

Bad: the orchestrator knows about everyone, so it's a coupling point and it changes whenever the process changes. It can become a bottleneck, and if it's poorly designed it turns into a god service with all the business logic.

ChooseWhen
ChoreographyFew steps, no compensation needed, consumers are genuinely independent reactions (analytics, notifications, cache invalidation)
OrchestrationA multi-step business process, order matters, failures need compensation, someone will ask "where is order 123?"
The practical rule

If a human will ever ask "what state is this process in?" — orchestrate it.

Choreography makes that question expensive to answer, and you'll be asked it during an incident.

The saga pattern covers both variants, including compensation.

What event-driven actually costs ⭐ Senior-level

The benefits get quoted constantly. Here's the other side, which is what interviews probe.

CostWhat it means day to day
Eventual consistencyA user saves something and it isn't visible on the next screen. That's a product problem, not just technical
Duplicate deliveryAt-least-once is the norm. Every consumer must be idempotent or you double-charge someone
Out-of-order arrivalOrderCancelled can arrive before OrderPlaced. Handlers must tolerate it

Production trap — the one that always bites

Assuming exactly-once delivery. It doesn't exist in practice across a network — you get at-least-once, and you make it behave like exactly-once by making handlers idempotent.

Concretely: store the processed message ID, and if you've seen it, skip. Details in idempotency.

Designing events well 🟡 Intermediate

  1. Past tense, business language. InvoicePaid, not InvoiceTableUpdated. Events describe business facts, not database operations.
  2. Include an event ID and a timestamp. The ID is what makes idempotency possible; the timestamp is what makes ordering debuggable.
  3. Include a correlation ID flowing from the original request, so you can follow one user action across every service.
  4. Version from day one. Either in the type name (OrderPlacedV2) or in a header. Retrofitting versioning is painful.
  5. Only additive changes. Add optional fields; never remove or repurpose one. Consumers on old code must keep working.
  6. Don't leak internals. Publishing your EF entity as the event means every internal rename becomes a breaking change for other teams.
public sealed record OrderPlacedV1(
    Guid EventId,              // for idempotency
    DateTime OccurredAtUtc,    // for ordering and debugging
    Guid CorrelationId,        // ties back to the originating request
    Guid OrderId,
    Guid CustomerId,
    decimal Total,
    string Currency);          // primitives only - this is serialized and versioned

When not to go event-driven

  • Between two classes in the same process. A method call is simpler, traceable and transactional. Use in-process domain events if you want decoupling — not a broker.
  • When the caller needs an answer. If you need a result to continue, that's request/reply. Faking it with events plus a correlation timeout is worse than an HTTP call.
  • When the operation must be transactional. Events cross the transaction boundary by definition.
  • When the team lacks the operational base. Without distributed tracing, centralised logging, DLQ monitoring and replay tooling, event-driven turns every bug into an archaeology project.
Key takeaways
  • Command = one handler, refusable. Event = many handlers, already happened, past tense.
  • Thin events couple you on availability; fat events couple you on schema. Pick deliberately.
  • Choreography for independent reactions; orchestration when someone will ask "where is this process?"
  • At-least-once is the reality. Idempotent handlers are mandatory, not optional.
  • Every event needs an ID, a timestamp and a correlation ID.
  • Events are a public contract — additive changes only.

Common mistakes

  • Commands published as events (SendEmail on a topic), giving you the coupling of a command with the untraceability of an event.
  • Publishing internal entities as event payloads, so every rename breaks consumers.
  • No idempotency, so a redelivered message double-charges or double-ships.
  • No correlation ID, making cross-service debugging effectively impossible.
  • No dead-letter monitoring — messages fail silently for weeks.
  • Events for everything, including two components in the same process that could have used a method call.
  • Assuming ordering because it worked in testing with one consumer.

Interview questions

What's the difference between a command and an event?
How I'd answer

"A command tells a specific handler to do something — it's imperative, there's exactly one handler, and it can be rejected. An event says something already happened — past tense, any number of subscribers, and it can't be refused because it's a statement of fact."

"The practical difference is in coupling and in what happens when requirements change. With a command, the sender knows the receiver. With an event, the publisher doesn't know who's listening, so you can add a consumer without touching the publisher at all."

"The mistake I look for in reviews is a command dressed as an event — something like SendWelcomeEmail published to a topic. It's imperative, one thing should handle it, and the publisher clearly does know what should happen. The event version is UserRegistered, and then the email service decides to send a welcome email. That's what lets analytics subscribe later without a change."

"There's a design consequence too. Because an event can't be rejected, anything that needs to be validated or refused has to be a command. If I find myself wanting to 'reject' an event, my model is wrong — the thing hasn't actually happened yet."

What they're really checking
  • Do you know the naming convention and why it isn't cosmetic?
  • Do you understand the coupling difference?
  • Can you spot a command-as-event in a design?
Likely follow-ups
  • Which transport would you use for each — queue or topic?
  • How much data should an event carry?
  • What if two services both need to handle the same command?
Choreography or orchestration for a multi-step order process? ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"For a genuine multi-step business process with compensation, I'd orchestrate — and the deciding question for me is: will anyone ever ask 'where is order 123 right now?' For an order flow, someone absolutely will, probably during an incident."

"With choreography, that question is expensive. The process doesn't exist anywhere — it's an emergent property of six services each reacting. To answer it you check six sets of logs and infer what happened. And if the flow needs to change, you change several services."

"With orchestration, the process is a readable piece of code in one place. You can query the orchestrator for the current state. And compensation has an obvious home — if shipping fails after payment succeeded, the orchestrator knows to trigger a refund. In choreography that logic gets scattered, or forgotten."

"The cost is real though: the orchestrator knows about everyone, so it's a coupling point and it changes whenever the process changes. The way I'd contain that is to keep it thin — it sequences steps and handles failures, but the business rules stay in the services. If the orchestrator starts containing pricing logic, it's become a god service."

"I'd still use choreography for the things that are genuinely independent reactions — analytics, notifications, cache invalidation, search indexing. Those aren't steps in a process; they're side effects nobody is waiting on, and coupling them into an orchestrator adds nothing."

"So in practice most systems are both: an orchestrated core process, with choreographed reactions hanging off the events it emits."

What they're really checking
  • Do you weigh operability, not just coupling?
  • Do you know compensation needs a home?
  • Do you recognise most real systems use both?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: "choreography, because it's more decoupled" with no mention of debugging or compensation.
  • Red flag: not knowing what compensation means.
Likely follow-ups
  • How do you implement the orchestrator so it survives a restart mid-process?
  • What happens if a compensating action itself fails?
  • How would you monitor a stuck saga?
What are the downsides of event-driven architecture?
How I'd answer

"The one that surprises teams most is loss of traceability. One user action becomes a dozen messages across services, and without correlation IDs and distributed tracing from day one, answering 'what happened to this order?' becomes archaeology. That's a bigger day-to-day cost than the consistency issues people usually mention first."

"Then eventual consistency, which is genuinely a product problem, not just a technical one. A user saves something and it isn't on the next screen. Somebody has to decide whether that's acceptable, and if it isn't, you need UI patterns to handle it — optimistic display, a pending state, or reading your own writes from the source."

"Duplicate and out-of-order delivery. At-least-once is the reality, so every consumer has to be idempotent — and I'd say that unprompted, because it's where real money gets lost. Out-of-order matters too: a cancellation can arrive before the placement."

"Schema versioning. Events are a published contract. Once six services read a field, you can't remove it without coordinating six deployments. So additive-only changes, and versioning designed in up front."

"And an operational one people underestimate: poison messages and dead-letter queues. Without monitoring on the DLQ, messages fail silently for weeks and you find out from a customer."

"So my summary would be that event-driven trades compile-time and transactional guarantees for flexibility. It's a good trade at real boundaries between teams or services. It's a bad trade between two classes in the same process, where a method call gives you the same decoupling with none of the cost."

What they're really checking
  • Have you operated one, or only read about it?
  • Does idempotency come up unprompted?
  • Do you recognise eventual consistency as a product decision?
Likely follow-ups
  • How do you make a consumer idempotent?
  • How do you handle a poison message?
  • How do you version an event that six services consume?
