Architecture & Integration

How systems get structured, how the pieces talk, and what keeps them alive when parts of them fail.

🟡 Intermediate 🔴 Advanced ⭐ Senior-level
Quick revision
  • Architecture answers three questions: what are the pieces, how do they talk, and where does the data live?
  • Every architecture style is a different answer to "what do we want to be able to change independently?"
  • The moment you split a system across a network, you inherit partial failure, latency and eventual consistency. Everything in this section is about paying that bill.
  • Layered, Clean, Hexagonal and Onion are variations of one idea: dependencies point inward, toward the stable business rules.
  • You almost never need distributed architecture for scale. You need it for independent deployability — which is an organisational need.
  • Name the trade-off you're accepting. Every architecture makes something worse.

What architecture actually decides 🟡 Intermediate

Remember this

Architecture is the set of decisions that are expensive to reverse — and almost all of them are about boundaries.

Three questions cover most of it:

  1. What are the pieces? Modules, services, layers. Where does one end and the next begin? (Application architectures, DDD.)
  2. How do they talk? Synchronous call or message? Request/reply or fire-and-forget? Who knows about whom? (Integration patterns, event-driven architecture.)
  3. Where does the data live, and who owns it? One database or many? What happens when two components need the same data? (Distributed data.)

And then a fourth question that only shows up in production: what happens when a piece fails? (Resilience patterns.)

The trade-off underneath everything ⭐ Senior-level

Almost every architectural argument reduces to one axis:

Fewer, larger piecesMore, smaller pieces
Change spanning componentsOne commit, one deploy, one transactionCoordinated across teams and releases
ConsistencyTransactions do it for youYou implement it — sagas, outbox, idempotency
DebuggingOne stack traceDistributed tracing, correlation IDs, several dashboards
Independent deploymentNoYes — this is the actual reason to split
Independent scalingScale the whole thingScale the hot part only
Failure isolationOne bad component can take everything downContained — if you built bulkheads and timeouts
Team autonomyCoordination on shared codeTeams own their service end to end
Senior signal

Saying that splitting is primarily an organisational decision, not a technical one. Most systems that "need microservices for scale" could scale fine as a monolith on bigger hardware. What they actually can't do is let eight teams deploy independently.

Getting that distinction right is one of the clearest markers of architectural experience.

What changes the moment you cross a network 🔴 Advanced

This is the bill you pay for distribution, and it's worth being able to list:

  • Partial failure. An in-process call either works or throws. A network call can also hang, time out, succeed-but-you-didn't-hear, or succeed twice.
  • No shared transaction. You can't roll back a call another service already committed. That's why sagas exist.
  • Latency stops being free. A method call is nanoseconds. A network call is milliseconds. Do it in a loop and you've built an N+1 across the network.
  • Ordering isn't guaranteed. Messages arrive out of order, or twice.
  • Contracts become versioned. You can't change a shared type and recompile — the other side is already deployed.

Every pattern in this section exists because of one of those five.

How to choose an architecture

A practical order of questions, rather than starting from a diagram:

  1. How many teams need to deploy independently? One team → a modular monolith is almost always right. Many teams with separate release cadences → the case for services starts to exist.
  2. What must be strongly consistent? Anything that must be transactionally consistent should live in one service, in one database. Splitting it is how you end up implementing distributed transactions by hand.
  3. What actually needs to scale differently? Usually one or two components. Those are candidates for extraction — not everything.
  4. What's the team's operational maturity? Distributed systems require tracing, centralised logging, deployment automation and on-call. Without those, splitting makes things worse, not better.
  5. What's genuinely likely to change? Put boundaries where change happens, not where the layers diagram suggests.

Cross-cutting interview questions

How do you decide on an architecture for a new system?
How I'd answer

"I start from constraints rather than from a style. The questions that actually determine the answer are: how many teams will work on this, what has to be strongly consistent, what needs to scale differently, and what's the team's operational maturity."

"For most new systems my default is a modular monolith — one deployable, but with hard internal boundaries between modules, each owning its own data and talking through explicit interfaces. That gives me most of the design benefits of services with none of the distributed cost, and it keeps the option open: if a module later needs independent deployment, the seam is already there."

"I'd only start distributed if there's a concrete reason on day one — several teams with conflicting release cadences, a component with wildly different scaling needs, or a regulatory requirement to isolate something."

"The thing I try hardest to get right early is where the boundaries are, because that's the expensive decision. Anything that must be transactionally consistent stays together. Boundaries go where the business language changes, which is really the bounded context idea from DDD."

"And I'd name what I'm trading away. A monolith means the whole thing deploys together and one bad component can affect the rest, so I'd want good module isolation and testing. Saying that out loud matters — an architecture presented with no downsides is usually one that hasn't been thought through."

What they're really checking
  • Do you reason from constraints, or arrive with a favourite style?
  • Do you understand that boundaries are the expensive decision?
  • Do you state trade-offs unprompted?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: "microservices" as a default for a new system with one team.
  • Red flag: naming a layered diagram without mentioning data ownership or failure.
Likely follow-ups
  • How do you stop a modular monolith turning into a big ball of mud?
  • When would you extract the first service, and which one?
What changes when two components are separated by a network? ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"Five things, and each one creates work you didn't have before."

"Partial failure. An in-process call either returns or throws. A network call can also hang, time out, or succeed without you hearing the response. That last one is the nasty case, because retrying might duplicate the effect — which is why idempotency stops being optional."

"No shared transaction. You can't roll back something the other service already committed. So you either keep the things that must be consistent together, or you accept eventual consistency and implement compensation — a saga."

"Latency becomes real. A method call is nanoseconds; a network call is milliseconds. If you call it inside a loop you've built an N+1 across the network, and that's one of the most common performance failures in service architectures."

"Ordering isn't guaranteed. Messages can arrive out of order or twice, so handlers have to tolerate both."

"Contracts become versioned. You can't change a shared type and recompile — the other side is already deployed and running. Every change has to be backwards compatible for at least one release."

"So my summary would be: distribution is not a free way to organise code. It converts compiler errors into runtime failures, and it converts transactions into engineering work."

What they're really checking
  • Have you actually operated a distributed system, or only drawn one?
  • Do you connect each consequence to the pattern that addresses it?
Likely follow-ups
  • How do you make a handler idempotent?
  • What's the difference between a saga and a distributed transaction?
  • How would you find an N+1 across services?
