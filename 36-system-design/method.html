How to Run a Design Interview

Structure is most of the score. A well-organised design with one gap beats a brilliant idea delivered as a stream of consciousness.

🔴 Advanced 🔥 Must know
Quick revision
  • Requirements first, always. Ten minutes, not two.
  • Write down what you're excluding. Scope control is a scored skill.
  • Estimate to size decisions, not to show arithmetic.
  • Data model before architecture. The data drives the boxes.
  • Name a trade-off on every significant choice.
  • Volunteer the failure modes. Don't wait to be asked.
  • Finish with operability — monitoring, deployment, what you'd alert on.
  • Say the reasoning out loud. Silent thinking scores nothing.

40-45  OPERABILITY       Monitoring, alerts, deployment, migrations.

Draw the boxes, but not first

The instinct is to start drawing an architecture diagram, because that's what a design looks like. It's the wrong order: the boxes are a consequence of the requirements and the data model, so drawing them first means you've decided before you know anything.

A candidate who draws an API gateway, three services, a queue and a cache in the first two minutes has produced a generic diagram that would fit any question. Interviewers see it constantly and it signals pattern-matching rather than reasoning.

Better: agree the requirements, work out what data you're storing and how it's accessed, and let the components fall out of that. Then draw, and the diagram is defensible because every box exists for a stated reason.

When you do draw, keep it simple and walk a request through it. Tracing one concrete operation end to end — what receives it, what validates it, what persists it, what responds — is far more convincing than a diagram with more boxes.

Senior signal — the consistency question is usually the pivotal one

If you only ask a few questions, make one of them "what has to be strongly consistent, and what can be eventually consistent?"

It determines more of the design than scale does. Strong consistency on a specific invariant means a single writer, a transaction, or an atomic conditional write — and constrains how you partition and replicate. Tolerating eventual consistency opens up queues, read replicas, caching, CQRS and independent services.

And the answer is almost never uniform across a system. In a booking platform, seat allocation must be strongly consistent — double-booking is unacceptable — while the search index can be seconds stale and nobody minds. Recognising that different parts of one system get different guarantees is a strong signal on its own, and it lets you spend strong consistency only where it's needed.

The related question worth asking: "what's the cost of being wrong here?" A stale product description costs nothing. A stale stock level oversells. A stale permission check is a security incident. That framing gets you to the right guarantee quickly and shows you're reasoning about consequences rather than applying a default.

Write down the exclusions, explicitly

"I'm going to assume authentication is handled by an existing identity provider and not design it. I'll cover reporting only briefly. I'm treating the payment provider as a black box."

Saying that does three useful things. It demonstrates scope control, which is a scored skill — shipping anything real requires deciding what not to build. It lets you go deep on what matters instead of shallow on everything, and depth is what distinguishes answers. And it gives the interviewer a chance to redirect: "actually, I'd like you to cover the reporting side."

That last point matters more than it seems. The interviewer usually has a specific area they want to probe, and stating your plan lets them steer you there rather than waiting while you spend twenty minutes elsewhere.

The failure mode is trying to cover everything: authentication, reporting, admin, mobile, analytics — each for ninety seconds. That reads as inability to prioritise, and there's nothing for the interviewer to dig into.

Estimate to reach a conclusion, then stop

Estimation is a means, not a display. The mark isn't for the arithmetic — it's for saying what the number implies.

"About 120 writes a second at peak — that's comfortable for a single well-indexed SQL database, so I don't need to shard and I'll design for a single primary" is the useful form. It has converted a number into a decision and closed off a whole branch of complexity.

"Roughly 10,000 reads a second, which is beyond what I'd want to hit the database directly, so caching is load-bearing rather than an optimisation" is the same move on the read side, and it tells you where to spend the rest of your time.

Two habits. Round aggressively — 86,400 seconds is 100,000, a million users is a million; the order of magnitude is what decides architecture, and precision wastes time. And state your assumptions as you go, so a wrong assumption gets corrected rather than invalidating everything after it.

And be ready to say when the numbers make scale a non-issue. Plenty of real systems serve a few hundred requests a second, and a candidate who designs a globally-distributed event-sourced platform for that has misread the problem. Recognising that the boring answer is sufficient is a genuine senior signal.

Senior signal — "what must be transactional together" defines the services

This is the most useful question in the whole exercise, and most candidates never ask it.

If two pieces of data must change atomically, they should almost certainly live in one database owned by one service. Splitting them across services means you've replaced a transaction with a saga — compensation logic, eventual consistency, and a whole class of partial-failure states — for no benefit.

So transactional boundaries are a much better guide to service boundaries than either organisational structure or nouns in the requirements. Working outward from "what has to be consistent" produces boundaries that hold; working from "we have orders, customers and products, so three services" produces boundaries that need distributed transactions to function.

It's also how you justify not splitting. "Inventory and orders must be consistent when a purchase is confirmed, so I'd keep them in one service rather than run a saga for the core flow" is a strong answer, and it's the opposite of what a candidate reaching for microservices would say.

The related irreversible decision is the partition key. In Cosmos DB and DynamoDB it can't be changed without a migration, and it sets both the scale ceiling and which queries are cheap. Naming it explicitly — and saying what you'd need to know to choose it, particularly the size distribution — is a strong signal.

Trade-offs 🔥 Must know

Instead ofSay
"I'd use a queue here." "A queue here, so a spike grows a backlog instead of timing out. The cost is that the caller gets a 202 and needs a way to check status."
"I'd cache this." "Cache this for five minutes. Stale prices for five minutes are acceptable; stale stock levels wouldn't be, so I'd invalidate those on write."
"Cosmos DB." "Cosmos, partitioned by tenant — but I'd want the largest tenant's volume first, because a very large one hits per-partition limits and adding RUs doesn't help."
"Microservices." "A modular monolith first. Independent deployability is the one real benefit and we don't need it yet; I'd enforce module boundaries so splitting later is mechanical."
Every choice has a cost — name it before you're asked

A design presented as a series of obviously-correct decisions reads as inexperience, because real designs are compromises. Interviewers probe precisely to find out whether you know what you gave up.

So volunteer it. The pattern is: choice, reason, cost, and when you'd choose differently. That last clause is the strongest part — "if writes grew past a few thousand a second I'd revisit this" shows you know the boundary of your own decision.

It also makes the conversation collaborative rather than adversarial. If you've already named the weakness, the interviewer engages with your reasoning instead of hunting for a gap — and you've set the agenda.

Two costs worth naming habitually because candidates rarely do: operational complexity — every component is something to monitor, deploy, patch and debug at 3am — and the cost of being wrong, which is what irreversibility means. A partition key choice and a caching TTL are not equally serious mistakes, and treating them the same misses something important.

Failure modes 🔥 Must know

What breaks first under load?A specific component, and why

Volunteering failure modes is the clearest experience signal available

A candidate who designs a happy path and waits to be asked about failure looks like someone who has built systems but not run them. A candidate who says "the risk here is that the payment provider succeeds and our write fails, so..." has obviously been on call.

The four that come up most often across every design, worth having ready:

Duplicate delivery. At-least-once is the norm, so any operation with a real-world side effect needs an idempotency mechanism — enforced by a database constraint, not an application-level check, because check-then-act is itself a race.

A slow dependency. Worse than a dead one, because it exhausts your threads and takes down endpoints that don't even use it. Timeout, then circuit breaker, then bulkhead.

Partial failure in a multi-step operation. Prefer retry over compensation, keep irreversible steps last, and be clear that compensation is a new business action rather than a rollback.

Schema change with zero downtime. Both versions run simultaneously during any rolling deployment, so expand-and-contract across separate releases. This one is very often unasked and very often the thing that actually bites in production.

Senior signal — the best alert is a business metric that stopped

Most candidates who mention monitoring say "we'd monitor CPU and memory and set up alerts." That's a non-answer.

Technical alerts detect known failure modes. The most valuable alert detects the unknown ones: "no orders completed in the last hour." That single alert catches a broken payment integration, a failed deployment, a full disk, an expired certificate, a misconfigured DNS record — including causes you never anticipated and have no specific alert for.

Mentioning that is a strong differentiator because it's rare, and because it shows you're thinking about outcomes rather than components.

Two supporting points. Alert on symptoms users feel — error rate, p95 latency, availability checks — rather than on every exception, because an alert nobody reads is worse than none. And return a correlation id in every response, so a support conversation starts with an identifier that retrieves the full distributed trace rather than a timestamp and a guess.

Finishing on operability leaves the interviewer with the impression that you'd own this system after it shipped, which is exactly the question behind a senior interview.

Changing your mind when challenged is a positive signal

Interviewers push on decisions. Sometimes to test conviction, often because they've spotted a genuine problem.

The strong response is to engage with the substance: "you're right that a single writer limits throughput. If we needed more than a few thousand writes a second I'd partition by region — I chose the simpler option because the requirement was a few hundred."

That shows you know the boundary of your own decision. Defending a choice you now see is wrong is the worse outcome, because it suggests you'd do the same on a real team — and it's the behaviour that makes someone hard to work with.

Equally, "I don't know" followed by how you'd find out is a good answer. Nobody knows every service's limits. "I'd check the documented per-partition throughput before committing to this key" is honest and demonstrates method. Inventing a number is the thing that damages credibility, because the interviewer usually knows the real one.

Key takeaways
  • Requirements for the first eight minutes; write down the exclusions.
  • "What must be strongly consistent?" is usually the pivotal question.
  • Different parts of one system deserve different guarantees.
  • Estimate to reach a decision, then stop; round aggressively.
  • Say when the numbers make scale a non-issue.
  • Data model before architecture; the boxes follow from it.
  • "What must be transactional together?" defines service boundaries.
  • Name the partition key and what you'd need to choose it.
  • Choice, reason, cost, and when you'd choose differently.
  • Volunteer failure modes — duplicates, slow dependencies, partial failure, schema change.
  • Finish on operability; the best alert is a stopped business metric.
  • Return a correlation id, and put tenant id on telemetry.
  • Change your mind when challenged; say "I don't know" with a method.

Common mistakes

  • Drawing boxes before asking anything.
  • No stated scope, so the answer is shallow everywhere.
  • Estimation as arithmetic with no implication drawn.
  • Designing for scale that isn't required.
  • Microservices by default.
  • Splitting data that must be transactional.
  • No partition key decision, or one chosen without asking about distribution.
  • Presenting every choice as obvious.
  • Happy path only, waiting to be asked about failure.
  • "We'd monitor CPU and memory" as the operability answer.
  • No mention of deployment or migrations.
  • Silent thinking, then a conclusion with no visible reasoning.
  • Defending a choice after being shown it's wrong.
  • Inventing a number rather than saying you'd check.

How would you approach a system design question you've never seen before? 🔥 Must know
How I'd answer

"I'd follow the same structure regardless of the domain, because the structure is what makes an unfamiliar problem tractable — and it's also most of what's being assessed."

"First, requirements — and I'd spend real time here, seven or eight minutes of forty-five. Who uses it and for what, how many users and how active, read-heavy or write-heavy, and the two questions that matter most: what has to be strongly consistent, and what latency is acceptable."

"The consistency question determines more of the design than scale does. Strong consistency on a specific invariant means a single writer, a transaction or an atomic conditional write. Tolerating eventual consistency opens up queues, replicas, caching and independent services."

"And the answer is almost never uniform. In a booking system, seat allocation must be strongly consistent while search results can be seconds stale — so I'd spend strong consistency only where the cost of being wrong justifies it."

"Then I'd state my scope explicitly: what I'm covering and what I'm treating as out of scope. That demonstrates prioritisation, lets me go deep rather than shallow, and gives the interviewer a chance to redirect me to whatever they actually want to probe."

"Then estimation, but only enough to size decisions. The mark isn't for the arithmetic — it's for the implication. '120 writes a second at peak, which a single well-indexed SQL database handles easily, so I won't shard' converts a number into a decision and closes off a branch of complexity. I'd round aggressively, because the order of magnitude is what decides architecture."

"I'd also be willing to say when the numbers make scale a non-issue, because designing a globally-distributed platform for four hundred requests a second is misreading the problem."

"Then the data model, before any architecture diagram. Entities, access patterns, and specifically what must be transactional together — because that defines service boundaries far better than nouns in the requirements do. If two things must change atomically, they belong in one database owned by one service; splitting them means replacing a transaction with a saga for no benefit."

"I'd name the partition key explicitly and say what I'd need to know to choose it — particularly the size distribution, since a very skewed one creates a hot partition that capacity can't fix. In Cosmos or DynamoDB that choice is irreversible, so it deserves stating as such."

"Only then the components, and I'd walk one concrete request end to end rather than drawing a generic diagram. Tracing an actual operation — what receives it, validates it, persists it, responds — is much more convincing than a picture with more boxes. And I'd name a trade-off on every significant choice: the reason, the cost, and when I'd choose differently."

"Then failure modes, volunteered rather than waiting to be asked. What breaks first under load. What happens when a dependency is slow rather than down, which is worse because it exhausts threads and takes out unrelated endpoints. What happens on duplicate delivery, since at-least-once is the norm. What happens if a multi-step operation fails halfway."

"And I'd finish on operability, because that's what a senior interview is really asking: would you own this after it shipped? What I'd alert on — user-visible symptoms plus a business metric that stopped, which catches failures nobody anticipated. Correlation ids returned to clients. Tenant id on telemetry so 'one customer or everyone?' is answerable. How deployment and schema migration work with both versions running."

"Two things I'd hold to throughout. Think out loud, because silent reasoning scores nothing. And change my mind when shown a genuine problem — engaging with the substance is a much better signal than defending a choice I can see is wrong."

What they're really checking
  • Do you have a repeatable structure?
  • Do you prioritise requirements and consistency over diagrams?
  • Do you use estimation to reach decisions?
  • Do you derive boundaries from transactional needs?
  • Do you volunteer failure modes and operability?
  • Do you think out loud and stay open to correction?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: starting with an architecture diagram.
  • Red flag: no requirements-gathering phase.
  • Red flag: the same architecture for every question.
