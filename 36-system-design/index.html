Senior signal — the question is usually "have you run something like this?"

At seven years' experience, an interviewer isn't checking whether you can invent a consistent-hashing scheme. They're checking whether you've operated a real system and learned what hurts.

Which means the things that score are specific and unglamorous: how you handle a payment that succeeded but whose response was lost, what happens when the queue backs up, how you migrate the schema without downtime, how you tell whether one tenant is causing the problem, and what you'd have alerted on.

"We'd use Cosmos DB" is a technology choice. "We'd use Cosmos with tenant as the partition key, and I'd want to know the largest tenant's volume first because a very large one hits per-partition limits and adding RUs doesn't help" is experience. Same sentence length, entirely different signal.

So the material in these pages leans toward failure modes, consistency choices and operability rather than capacity arithmetic. That's where the marks are, and it's also where most candidates are thinnest.

These designs deliberately don't converge on one architecture

The booking system uses strong consistency and a relational database because double-booking is unacceptable. The notification platform is eventually consistent and queue-driven because a two-second delay is fine. The document pipeline is durable-orchestration-based because steps take minutes.

That's the point. A candidate who reaches for the same architecture regardless of requirements hasn't understood the requirements. The most common failure in design interviews isn't ignorance — it's applying a remembered pattern to a problem that doesn't need it.

So when reading these, pay attention to which requirement forced which decision. That's the transferable part. The specific choice of service matters much less than being able to say why an alternative was rejected.

Key takeaways
  • The senior .NET design interview rewards judgement and operability, not novel algorithms.
  • Managed services used well beats infrastructure built from scratch.
  • Specific failure modes and consistency choices are where the marks are.
  • Attempt each design before reading it — the gaps are the value.
  • Learn which requirement forced which decision, not the design itself.
  • Reaching for one architecture regardless of requirements is the common failure.
  • Unspoken reasoning scores nothing — say the trade-offs out loud.
