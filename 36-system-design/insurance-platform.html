Insurance Management Platform

A regulated domain, where the requirement that changes everything is being able to reconstruct what you knew and decided at any past moment.

Senior signal — the defining requirement is temporal, not technical

Most designs in this section are shaped by scale or consistency. This one is shaped by time, and recognising that changes the whole model.

A regulator or a court can ask: "on 14 March 2023, what did this policy cover, what did you know about the customer, and what rules did you apply?" Answering that requires more than an audit log of changes — it requires that the state and the logic at that moment are both reconstructable.

Which rules out the default design. A Policies table with an UPDATE and an audit trigger tells you a field changed; it doesn't let you reconstitute the policy as it stood, or apply the underwriting rules that were in force then.

So the model is append-only, temporal, and version-aware about rules as well as data. That's the decision everything else follows from, and stating it early is what distinguishes a design that meets the requirement from one that meets it approximately.

The compensating good news: volume is modest. Hundreds of thousands of policies means a single relational database is entirely adequate, so you can spend the complexity budget on correctness and history rather than on distribution.

Two dimensions of time 🔥 Must know

  A customer rings on 20 August to add a driver, effective from 1 August.

  EFFECTIVE (valid) time   when the change applies in the real world  -> 1 Aug
  RECORDED (system) time   when we learned about it                   -> 20 Aug

  Both are needed, and they answer different questions:

  "What did the policy cover on 10 August?"
       -> as we understand it TODAY: the driver was covered
       -> as we understood it ON 15 AUGUST: the driver was NOT covered

  The second question is the one that matters in a dispute, because it is
  what the premium was calculated on and what a claims handler saw.

  A single "LastUpdated" column can answer NEITHER.

Bi-temporal versioning, and why not event sourcing

My choice would be explicit append-only versioned rows carrying both ValidFrom/ValidTo (effective time) and RecordedAt (system time), rather than full event sourcing.

The reasoning: this gives the regulator-facing capability — reconstruct any state as at any pair of times — while keeping the model something a developer can query directly with SQL. The history is data, not a stream requiring a projection to interpret.

Event sourcing would also work and it's a much larger commitment: projections to build, rebuild and keep consistent, eventual consistency on every read model, schema evolution on events that must remain readable for a decade, and a team that has to understand it. For hundreds of thousands of policies, that's complexity without a proportionate benefit.

Being able to say why not event sourcing is a stronger signal than proposing it, because it's the obvious thing to reach for in this domain and the trade-off is rarely examined.

What I would use it for is one bounded context where the history genuinely is the domain — claims progression, perhaps — rather than for the whole system. That's the pragmatic middle: full history where it earns its cost.

One honest cost of the versioned approach: every query needs temporal predicates, and forgetting them returns duplicate rows or a superseded version. So access goes through a repository that applies them by default — the same structural-enforcement argument as a tenant filter.

"Why" is the field that gets left out, and the one that's asked for

Most audit implementations capture who, what and when — usually via a trigger or an EF Core interceptor diffing entities. That's mechanical and it misses the important part.

A regulator examining a declined claim doesn't primarily want to know that CoverLevel changed from Standard to Basic on a Tuesday. They want to know why: was it a customer request, an underwriting decision, a correction of a data-entry error, or a system migration?

Those have completely different implications, and a field-level diff cannot distinguish them.

Which means the audit should record the intent, not just the effect — the command that was issued, with its reason code and any note. That's a genuine argument for capturing changes as domain operations (DriverAdded, CoverDowngraded, DataCorrected) rather than as row diffs.

Two structural requirements. Append-only, enforced by permissions — the application's database user has INSERT but not UPDATE or DELETE on audit tables, so tampering isn't possible even through a bug or a compromised credential. And capture system-initiated changes with the same rigour: a batch job or an integration feed adjusting thousands of policies must be as attributable as a user click, or your audit has a large hole exactly where bulk changes happen.

Versioned business rules 🔥 Must know

  A claim from March 2023 is disputed in 2026.

  "Why was this declined?"
     -> because the exclusion rules AS AT MARCH 2023 excluded it
     -> the rules have since changed twice

  If the rules only exist as TODAY's deployed code, you cannot answer.
  Re-running the claim gives TODAY's answer, which is the wrong answer.

  So:
    - rule sets are DATA, versioned, with effective dates
    - every decision records WHICH rule version produced it
    - and ideally the INPUTS, so the decision is reproducible
ApproachReproducible?Trade-off
Record the decision and its inputs Yes — the decision itself is the record Storage; must capture every relevant input
Rule sets as versioned data Yes — re-evaluate against the version in force A rules engine to build and test
Rules in code, with version tags in git In principle, by checking out old code Not practical to run in production
Rules in code, unversioned NoCannot answer the question at all
Senior signal — record the decision, not just the outcome

The simplest thing that satisfies the requirement, and the one I'd do first: every automated decision writes an immutable record containing its inputs, the rule version applied, the outcome, and the reasoning — which conditions matched.

That answers "why was this declined in March 2023?" directly, without needing to re-run anything. The decision is the evidence.

It's also much cheaper than a full rules engine, and it composes with one: if rules are versioned data, the record references a rule set version and the decision can be re-derived; if rules are still in code, the record is at least self-contained.

The important discipline is capturing the inputs. A decision record saying "declined under exclusion 4b" is useful; one that also captures the claim details and customer data the decision saw is reproducible. Without inputs you're trusting that nothing else changed — and in a domain where the customer's record has itself been amended since, that assumption is wrong.

Which connects back to bi-temporal data: the decision was made against the data as it was recorded at that time, not as it appears now. Both mechanisms are needed for the same question, and that's the insight worth articulating.

For rules as data, the honest caution: a rules engine is often sold as "the business can change rules" and in practice needs the same testing and release discipline as code. So it's worth building for versioning and auditability, and being sceptical of the self-service promise.

Decide who owns each entity, or you get a fight

The failure that costs the most in legacy integration is two systems both believing they own the same data. Ours updates a policy, the nightly feed overwrites it with the mainframe's version, a user re-enters the change, and it flips back the next night.

Nothing errors. The data oscillates, users lose confidence in both systems, and diagnosing it takes a surprisingly long time because each system is behaving correctly by its own lights.

So ownership has to be explicit per entity, and written down: the mainframe owns policy administration, we own claims. Data flowing from a non-owner is read-only in the receiving system, enforced by not exposing an edit path — not by asking people to remember.

The anti-corruption layer is the second essential piece. Legacy systems have models shaped by decades of constraints — flags with three meanings, dates as strings, codes nobody can decode. Translating at the boundary keeps that out of your domain model, and it means the eventual migration changes one component rather than every class.

And design for the batch reality rather than pretending: if the feed is nightly, the UI should say when data was last synchronised. Showing a stale value as though it were current is worse than showing it with a timestamp.

Senior signal — store the rendered document, and know why

The tempting approach is to store the data and regenerate the policy document on demand. That's wrong here, and the reason is worth articulating.

The document the customer received is the contract. Regenerating it years later applies today's template, today's wording, today's branding — and possibly a template bug fixed since. What you produce is not what they were sent, which in a dispute is exactly the wrong answer.

So the rendered PDF is stored as issued, with a hash so tampering is detectable, in a container with an immutability policy — time-based retention that even an account owner cannot delete within the window. That's the only control an attacker with full credentials can't undo, and it's what makes the archive defensible rather than merely present.

On archive tiering: Archive is genuinely cheaper and it is not readable — rehydration takes hours. For documents a customer or a claims handler might request, Cool or Cold is the right tier. And the minimum-retention charges mean moving data to a colder tier too early can cost more.

The conflict worth raising unprompted: GDPR erasure versus regulatory retention. A customer asking to be forgotten does not override a statutory obligation to retain insurance records — Article 17 has exemptions for legal obligations. So the design needs a documented position per data category, and the ability to erase marketing data while retaining contractual records. Saying "we'd delete everything" is the wrong answer, and so is "we can't delete anything".

Domain model shape 🔴 Advanced

  POLICY aggregate            (append-only versions, bi-temporal)
    identity, cover, insured parties, premium, endorsements
    invariant: cover and premium must always be consistent with each other
    -> one aggregate, one transaction

  CLAIM aggregate             (its own lifecycle, its own history)
    references a POLICY VERSION - the one in force at the incident date
    invariant: reserve and payments must not exceed authority limits

  CUSTOMER aggregate
    identity, contact, relationships. Referenced by policies

  DECISION RECORDS            (immutable, append-only, never an aggregate)
    underwriting decisions, claims decisions - with inputs and rule version

  Separate: DOCUMENTS (blob + metadata), AUDIT (append-only), REPORTING (projection)
A claim references a policy version, not a policy

This is the detail that shows the temporal model is understood rather than bolted on.

A claim is assessed against the cover in force on the incident date — not today's cover. If the policy was amended after the incident, the claim must still be judged on what applied then. So the claim holds a reference to a specific policy version, resolved by effective date at the time the claim is created.

Get this wrong and claims silently change outcome when a policy is amended, which is both incorrect and a compliance problem — and it's the sort of bug that only surfaces during a dispute years later.

The related design point: the aggregate boundaries follow invariants. Cover and premium must be consistent with each other, so they're in one aggregate updated in one transaction. A claim's reserve and payments must respect authority limits, so those are one aggregate. Claims and policies are separate, because a claim being created doesn't need to be atomic with a policy amendment.

That's the same "what must be transactional together" question as everywhere else, and here it produces a relatively coarse-grained model — which is right for a domain where correctness matters far more than write throughput.

Reporting must not run against the transactional model

A bi-temporal append-only model is awful for reporting. Every query needs temporal predicates, there are many versions per entity, and an analyst writing SQL against it will get wrong answers — double-counting superseded versions is the obvious trap.

So reporting gets a projection: a flattened, current-state (or as-at-a-chosen-date) model built from the transactional data, in a separate database. Then analysts query something comprehensible and their queries don't compete with transactional work.

That's CQRS at its most justified — not for scale, but because the write model's shape is genuinely unsuitable for reading. Which is the honest reason to reach for it, and a better one than throughput.

The regulatory angle: reports must be reproducible. A report run today for last quarter should match the one run last quarter — so either the projection is itself as-at-dated, or reports are archived when produced. Otherwise a backdated amendment silently changes historical figures, and someone will notice during an audit.

Key takeaways
  • The defining requirement is temporal: reconstruct state and logic as at any past moment.
  • Two time dimensions — effective and recorded. A LastUpdated column answers neither.
  • Bi-temporal versioned rows over event sourcing, and be able to say why.
  • SQL temporal tables give system time only; effective time is still yours to model.
  • Enforce temporal predicates in a repository, not by remembering.
  • Audit must capture why, not just who/what/when.
  • Capture intent as domain operations rather than row diffs.
  • Audit tables append-only, enforced by database permissions.
  • Attribute system-initiated batch changes as rigorously as user actions.
  • Record every decision with its inputs and rule version — the decision is the evidence.
  • Be sceptical of the rules-engine self-service promise; build it for versioning.
  • Decide entity ownership explicitly, or the data oscillates.
  • An anti-corruption layer keeps the legacy model out of your domain.
  • Store the rendered document as issued, with immutability — don't regenerate.
  • Regulatory retention usually overrides GDPR erasure; document it per category.
  • A claim references a policy version, resolved by incident date.
  • Reporting needs a projection, and reports must be reproducible.
