Architecture & Technical Design

How design decisions get made, recorded and defended — and how to tell which ones actually deserve the effort.

🟡 Intermediate 🔴 Advanced ⭐ Senior-level
Quick revision
  • Architecture = the decisions that are expensive to reverse. Everything else is just design, and should be decided as late and cheaply as possible.
  • Classify every decision as a one-way or two-way door. Two-way: decide fast. One-way: slow down and write it up.
  • A design doc's value is in the alternatives you rejected and why.
  • ADRs — context, decision, consequences — are the cheapest useful documentation that exists.
  • Over-engineering isn't "too much code". It's flexibility bought before it's needed, paid for daily.
  • The senior move is naming the trade-off out loud and choosing anyway.

What counts as architecture 🟡 Intermediate

Remember this

Architecture is the subset of design decisions that are hard to change later — because other decisions rest on them, they're embedded in data, or other teams depend on them.

Whether you use a repository per aggregate is design. Whether orders and payments share a database is architecture.

DecisionHow reversible?Effort it deserves
Naming, file layout, internal class structureTrivialAlmost none — decide in the PR
Library for JSON, validation, mappingCheap if isolated behind your own typesMinutes; pick the boring one

Senior signal

Saying "that's a two-way door, let's pick one and move on" in a meeting that's been circling for twenty minutes is one of the highest-leverage things a senior engineer does.

Equally: recognising that an apparently small decision — a column type, an ID format, an event schema — is actually a one-way door, because data will accumulate under it.

The design document 🔥 Must know

A design doc exists to get disagreement to happen before the code is written, when it costs a comment instead of a rewrite.

Its second value: writing it forces you to discover the parts you hadn't thought about. The outline below is ordered so the hard parts can't be skipped.

1. Context and problem
   What is broken or needed, for whom, and what happens if we do nothing.
   Include numbers: current volume, growth, error rates, cost.

2. Goals / non-goals
   Non-goals are the more valuable half. They stop scope drift in review.

3. Constraints and assumptions
   Deadlines, existing systems, team skills, budget, compliance.
   Assumptions listed explicitly so a reviewer can challenge one.

4. Proposed design
   Components, data model changes, key flows INCLUDING failure flows,
   contract changes. Enough that someone else could implement it.

5. Alternatives considered
   At least two, each with why it lost. If you can't name a real
   alternative, you haven't designed - you've described your first idea.

6. Trade-offs accepted
   What gets worse. Every design makes something worse.

7. Risks and mitigations
   What could go wrong, how we'd detect it, what we'd do.

8. Rollout and rollback
   Migration steps, backwards compatibility, flags, how to undo it.

9. Observability
   What metrics/logs/traces are added, and what "healthy" looks like.

10. Open questions
    What you want the review to answer.

Two sections are the seniority tell:

  • Non-goals — shows you're managing scope rather than being pushed by it.
  • Trade-offs accepted — shows you know the design has costs. A doc that presents a solution with no downside reads as naive, and experienced reviewers immediately start hunting for what you missed.

Architecture Decision Records

ADRs are the lightweight persistent version: one short file per significant decision, stored next to the code, numbered, and never edited — superseded instead.

# 7. Use a transactional outbox for order events

Status: Accepted  (supersedes ADR-0004)
Date:   2025-03-11

## Context
Order state changes must reach the fulfilment service. Today we publish to
Service Bus inside the same method that saves the order. Last quarter we had
3 incidents where the DB commit succeeded and the publish failed, leaving
orders fulfilment never saw. Dual writes can't be made atomic across SQL and
Service Bus.

## Decision
Write the event to an Outbox table in the same SQL transaction as the order.
A background dispatcher polls the table and publishes, marking rows sent.
Consumers must be idempotent, keyed on event id.

## Consequences
+ No lost events: committed atomically with the state change.
+ Publish failures become retryable rather than data loss.
- Added latency: events land ~1s after commit, not instantly.
- New moving part: the dispatcher must be monitored (queue depth, age of
  oldest unsent row) and can itself fail.
- At-least-once delivery becomes explicit; every consumer now needs
  idempotency, which is work we previously avoided thinking about.

Notice the consequences section contains negatives. An ADR with only benefits is marketing.

Also notice the date and supersession. Six months later, "why is there a polling dispatcher in here?" has a permanent answer — which is what stops someone "simplifying" it back into the original bug. The pattern itself is covered in distributed data.

Reasoning about trade-offs 🔴 Advanced

Every architectural choice trades along a small number of recurring axes. Naming the axis is what turns a preference fight into a productive argument.

AxisYou buyYou pay with
Consistency ↔ availabilityAlways-correct readsUnavailability during partitions; higher latency
Latency ↔ durabilityFast acknowledgementRisk of losing recent writes on failure
Coupling ↔ autonomyIndependent deployabilityDuplicated data, eventual consistency, more ops
Simplicity ↔ flexibilityAbility to vary laterIndirection everyone pays to read, today
Performance ↔ maintainabilitySpeedCaching layers, denormalization, invalidation bugs
Build ↔ buyFit and controlMaintenance, security patching, the team's attention
Cost ↔ resilienceMulti-region, redundancyMoney and operational complexity
Trade-off — the one that causes most over-engineering

Flexibility is not free capacity held in reserve. It's complexity you pay for on every read, every debug session and every onboarding, in exchange for an option you may never exercise.

The rule that holds up: build the abstraction on the second concrete case, not the first. The first case can't tell you which axis will actually vary, so the abstraction you invent is usually the wrong one — and a wrong abstraction costs more than duplication.

What over-engineering actually looks like

It rarely looks like showing off. It looks like reasonable caution:

  • An interface with exactly one implementation, created "for testability" — when the concrete class was already injectable.
  • A repository over an ORM that's already an abstraction over the database, so every query passes through two layers that add nothing.
  • A message queue between two components that are always deployed together and always need a synchronous answer.
  • A plugin system for the one variation that exists.
  • Microservices at a team size where a modular monolith would ship faster and fail less.
The counter-test

"What specific, named, currently-plausible change does this make cheaper — and what is it costing me until then?"

If the answer is "any future change", it's speculative and probably wrong.

Spikes and prototypes 🟡 Intermediate

A spike is a timeboxed experiment answering a specific question. The discipline that makes it work: write the question and the timebox before starting, and agree the code is disposable.

Bad spike

"Investigate caching options."
No end date. Becomes a side project.

Good spike

"Can Redis sustain 8k reads/sec at p99 < 5ms on the smallest tier? One day, answer goes in the ticket."

Production trap

Prototype code shipping to production because "it already works" is one of the most reliable sources of long-term pain in this industry. Prototypes deliberately skip error handling, security, tests and edge cases — that's what made them fast.

If a prototype must be productionised, treat it as a rewrite with the prototype as the specification, and say so explicitly in the plan.

Running a design review

Reviews fail two ways: rubber-stamping, and bikeshedding the easy parts while the risky part goes unexamined. Both are avoidable:

  1. Circulate before the meeting. Reading live gets shallow feedback.
  2. Ask for specific feedback. "I'm most unsure about the migration path" directs attention at the real risk.
  3. Separate blocking from non-blocking comments. Naming preferences should never hold up a design.
  4. Invite the people who'll operate it, not just build it. The best feedback often comes from whoever carries the pager.
  5. Record the decision and the date. Undocumented outcomes get re-litigated.
Key takeaways
  • Sort decisions by reversibility first. That determines how much process they deserve.
  • The alternatives-rejected section is what makes a design doc worth writing.
  • An ADR without negative consequences isn't an ADR.
  • Name the trade-off axis out loud — it converts preference fights into design discussions.
  • Abstract on the second case, not the first.
  • Spikes need a written question and a timebox, or they become research projects.

Common mistakes

  • Designing for scale the business has never approached, while the current bottleneck goes unfixed.
  • Choosing technology by novelty. Every unusual choice is a hiring, on-call and support commitment for years.
  • No rollback plan for schema changes. Adding a column is easy; discovering the deploy can't be reverted because new code already wrote data old code can't read is a real outage.
  • Designing only the happy path, then discovering the failure design is the architecture.
  • Treating the design as fixed after review. Implementation always finds something. Update the doc; don't quietly diverge.
  • No observability in the design. If you can't answer "how will we know this works?", the design isn't finished.

Interview questions

How do you decide whether something needs a design document?
How I'd answer

"I use reversibility and blast radius. If the decision is cheap to undo and contained in one component, the pull request is the design document — writing a page first would just slow the feedback loop."

"If it changes a contract other teams depend on, changes the data model, introduces new infrastructure someone has to operate, or would be expensive to migrate away from — it gets written up."

"There's a second trigger that's about coordination rather than risk: if more than one person needs the same mental model to build it, or I expect disagreement, the document is the cheapest way to surface that early. Ninety minutes of writing that prevents two engineers building incompatible halves pays for itself many times over."

"Length should match the stakes. Most of what I write is one page — context, decision, consequences, effectively an ADR. Full multi-page designs are for genuinely architectural changes, maybe a handful a year."

"The failure mode I actively avoid is heavyweight process on small changes. That teaches the team documentation is a tax, and then nobody writes anything when it actually matters."

What they're really checking
  • Do you scale process to risk, or apply it uniformly?
  • Do you see documentation as coordination rather than compliance?
Likely follow-ups
  • What do you do when the design changes during implementation?
  • How do you stop design docs going stale?
Describe a design decision you got wrong. ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"The structure that works here: what you chose, what made it seem right at the time, what signal revealed it was wrong, what it cost, and what rule you now apply."

"For example: I introduced a generic rules engine so business users could configure pricing logic without deployments. The reasoning was sound on paper — pricing changed often and each change was a release."

"What actually happened was that the rules became a programming language with no debugger, no tests and no version control. Nobody outside the team ever wrote a rule, so we got all of the complexity and none of the benefit. Debugging a pricing bug meant reading data rows instead of code, and it took three times as long."

"What I took from it, as a rule: I now ask who specifically will use the flexibility, by name, and whether they've asked for it. If the answer is a hypothetical user, I build the concrete thing and keep the code easy to change. And configuration that replaces code needs the same tooling as code — otherwise you've built a worse programming language."

What they're really checking
  • Can you admit a real mistake with real consequences?
  • Did you extract a transferable principle, or just "I should have tested more"?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: a mistake that's secretly a virtue ("I cared too much about quality").
  • Red flag: blaming other people or changing requirements.
  • Red flag: a trivial example that carried no cost.
Likely follow-ups
  • How did you unwind it? What did the migration look like?
  • Would you make the same call at a different company size?
A teammate proposes a design you think is over-engineered. How do you handle the review?
How I'd answer

"I'd start by assuming they know something I don't, because in a review that's often true — there may be a roadmap item or a constraint that makes the flexibility justified. So my first move is a question, not a verdict: 'what change is this designed to make cheap, and when do we expect it?'"

"If there's a concrete near-term answer, the design may be right and I've learned something."

"If the answer is speculative, I make the cost visible rather than arguing principles. Concretely: this adds two indirections a new engineer has to traverse to follow a request, it makes stack traces harder to read, it adds a configuration surface we have to test combinations of, and it'll take an extra week we could spend on something we know is needed."

"Framing it as a cost paid daily against a benefit that's uncertain usually shifts the conversation."

"Then I'd offer the alternative rather than just objecting — usually: build the direct version now, keep the seam where variation would go, add the abstraction when the second case arrives. That respects their concern without paying for it upfront."

"And I'd hold it loosely. If they still disagree and it's a two-way door, it's not worth blocking on. Being right about a reversible decision is worth much less than the trust you spend winning the argument."

What they're really checking
  • Can you disagree technically without damaging the relationship?
  • Do you distinguish decisions worth fighting for from ones that aren't?
Likely follow-ups
  • What if it's a one-way door and you still disagree after discussion?
  • How do you review a design from someone much more senior than you?
