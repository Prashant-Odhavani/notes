Estimation & Planning

Why estimates are systematically wrong, what to do about it, and how to talk about dates without either lying or refusing to answer.

🟡 Intermediate ⭐ Senior-level
Quick revision
  • An estimate is a range, not a number. Giving a single number throws away the only information that mattered.
  • Estimates are wrong mostly because of unknown work, not slow typing.
  • Commitment ≠ forecast. A team commits to a sprint it controls; it forecasts a quarterly date it doesn't.
  • Story points measure relative size and uncertainty. Convert them to hours and they become a worse unit of time.
  • Little's Law: cycle time = WIP ÷ throughput. Cutting work-in-progress is usually the fastest way to deliver sooner.
  • Hidden padding gets consumed anyway. Explicit, attributed buffers survive scrutiny and actually work.

Why estimates are wrong 🟢 Beginner

Knowing the mechanisms is what lets you improve estimates instead of just apologising for them.

CauseWhat's actually happening
Cone of uncertainty You're asked to be most precise at the point you know least. Early estimates are routinely off by 2–4× either way. That's a property of the information available, not of skill.
Planning fallacy People estimate the path where nothing goes wrong, because that's the path they can picture. Integration surprises, flaky environments and the third round of feedback are invisible.
Unknown unknowns The dominant factor. You can't estimate work you don't know exists — you can only bound how long you'll spend discovering it.
Ignoring non-dev time Review, CI, QA cycles, deployment windows, meetings, support duty. A "3-day" task takes 8 elapsed days for entirely predictable reasons.
Anchoring Once someone says "feels like a week", every later estimate clusters around it. That's why planning poker reveals estimates simultaneously.

Remember this

"I can't estimate this yet" is a finding, not a failure.

Say: "The unknown is whether the vendor API supports partial refunds. Give me a day to find out and I'll come back with a range." A one-day spike that collapses a two-week uncertainty is the highest-return activity in planning.

Story points vs hours 🔥 Must know

Story pointsTime estimates
MeasuresRelative size + complexity + uncertaintyDuration
Stable across people?Yes — a 5 is a 5 whoever does itNo — varies by experience
Good forSprint capacity; spotting stories that are too bigFixed-scope external commitments
Fails whenUsed to compare teams or as a productivity metricUncertainty is high — single numbers imply false precision

The honest position: points work because they dodge the false precision of hours, and because humans are genuinely better at relative comparison than absolute duration.

They stop working the moment anyone outside the team consumes them as a performance number. Then they inflate and become useless. If your organisation does that, forecasting by story count and throughput is more robust — counts are harder to inflate.

Breaking work down ⭐ Senior-level

Accuracy improves far more from decomposition than from thinking harder. Splitting a 10-point story into five doesn't just distribute the estimate — it forces you to articulate the steps, and that's where forgotten work surfaces.

Ways to split, roughly in order of how often they apply:

  • By workflow step — create, then edit, then delete.
  • By rule variation — happy path now, the three exception rules as follow-ups.
  • By data variation — one file format now, the other two later.
  • By interface — API first, UI second, so integration risk shows up early.
  • Walking skeleton — the thinnest end-to-end path, deployed to production, before any depth. This is the single most effective risk-reduction technique available: it converts integration and deployment unknowns into known work in the first days rather than the last.
  • Spike then build — separate the learning from the delivery, and estimate only the delivery.
Common mistake

Splitting horizontally — a "database layer" story, a "service layer" story, a "UI" story. Looks tidy. Gives you three stories, none of which can be demonstrated, tested end to end or released — and defers every integration risk to the moment they're joined.

If a split doesn't produce something that could in principle ship, it's a task list, not stories.

Velocity, capacity and flow 🟡 Intermediate

Velocity is average completed points per sprint. Its only legitimate use is the team's own forecasting — and as a range (last five sprints' min and max), not an average, because the average hides the variability that is the actual planning risk.

Flow metrics are usually better for diagnosing why delivery is slow:

MetricDefinitionWhat it tells you
Cycle timeStarted → doneHow long work takes once begun. The number to quote to stakeholders
Lead timeRequested → doneWhat the customer experiences, including queueing
ThroughputItems completed per weekBasis for count-based forecasting
WIPItems in progressThe lever you can actually pull
Flow efficiencyActive time ÷ elapsed timeUsually shockingly low (10–25%) — proof that waiting dominates
Remember this — Little's Law

cycle time = WIP ÷ throughput

Six people each with two things in progress, team finishes six items a week → average cycle time is two weeks. Halving WIP halves cycle time without anyone working faster. "Stop starting, start finishing" isn't a slogan — it's arithmetic.

Communicating estimates honestly ⭐ Senior-level

How you present an estimate matters more than its accuracy, because the presentation determines what decisions get made on top of it.

Instead ofSayWhy it's better
"About three weeks.""Two to five weeks. The variance is the vendor's sandbox — if it behaves like the docs say, we're at the low end."Names the uncertainty and what would remove it
"I can't estimate that.""Not yet. One day to prototype the integration, then I can give you a range."Offers a path instead of a refusal
"We'll try.""With current scope we'll miss by about a week. If we drop bulk import, we make it."Gives the decision-maker a lever
Padding silently by 40%"Estimate is 8 days; I'd plan for 12 given the integration risk."The buffer survives scrutiny because it's attributed
Production trap — why hidden padding fails twice

First: everyone pads, so the total becomes absurd and management applies a blanket cut — which punishes the honest estimators.

Second: work expands to fill the padded time, so you get the schedule risk anyway with none of the safety.

Key takeaways
  • Separate known work from unknown work. Estimate the first, spike the second.
  • Decomposition improves accuracy more than any technique.
  • Include review, QA cycles, deployment and cross-team waits — often 40% of elapsed time.
  • Give ranges with the driver of the variance named.
  • Cut WIP to cut cycle time. It costs nothing.
  • Re-forecast as information arrives. An estimate at 10% done shouldn't survive to 50% done.

Common mistakes

  • Estimating alone and defending it later, instead of estimating with the people doing the work.
  • Treating a forecast as a commitment the moment it appears on a slide.
  • Estimating only development.
  • Re-estimating in-flight work to make the burndown look right. Burndown is a signal, not a target.
  • Assuming six people deliver six times one person. Coordination cost grows roughly with the square of team size — adding people to a late project usually makes it later.
  • Never comparing estimates to actuals. Without that loop, estimation can't improve.

Interview questions

How do you estimate a piece of work?
How I'd answer

"I start by separating what I know from what I don't, because they need different treatment."

"For the known part I decompose until each piece is small enough that I can picture the change. If I can't describe the files I'd touch, it's still too big to estimate."

"For the unknown part I don't estimate at all — I propose a timeboxed spike with a specific question. A number attached to unknown work is fiction, and everyone downstream treats it as fact."

"Then I deliberately include the non-coding work: review turnaround, test writing, the QA cycle with at least one round of fixes, migration scripts, deployment, and any coordination with another team. Cross-team dependencies are the biggest source of elapsed-time blowout, because they introduce queueing I don't control."

"I present it as a range with the driver of the variance named, and I say what would narrow it. And I re-forecast as information arrives — an estimate given at 10% done shouldn't survive unchanged to 50%. Honestly, the biggest accuracy improvement isn't a technique, it's decomposition plus actually looking back at what similar work took last time."

What they're really checking
  • Do you distinguish uncertainty from effort?
  • Do you account for the non-coding two-thirds?
  • Is estimation a feedback loop for you, or a one-off ritual?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: a confident single number for anything non-trivial.
  • Red flag: "I add 20% buffer" as the entire method.
  • Red flag: "estimates are pointless" — real teams still need "roughly when?"
Likely follow-ups
  • Your estimate was 2 weeks and it took 6. What did you learn?
  • How do you estimate work in a system you've never seen?
The deadline is fixed and the scope won't fit. What do you do?
How I'd answer

"The classic framing is scope, time, quality and people — pick three. Practically, adding people mid-project makes it later, and cutting quality is borrowing at a brutal rate because the defects come back inside the same release window. So the real lever is scope."

"My job is to make that conversation as easy as possible for whoever owns the deadline. So I'd arrive with a proposal, not a problem: split the scope into what must ship for the deadline to mean anything, what could ship two weeks later without harm, and what got included because it seemed convenient. Usually 60–70% of the value sits in a subset that fits."

"If the deadline is a genuine external commitment — a regulatory date, a conference — I'd also look for ways to meet the letter of it with a reduced but complete experience: fewer supported formats, a manual admin process instead of a UI, one region instead of three."

"What I wouldn't do is quietly compromise on the things that are invisible in a demo and expensive later — error handling, data migration correctness, and the ability to roll back. Those aren't polish. If I'm pushed to cut them, I'd name the specific risk being accepted and who's accepting it."

"And I'd raise all of this as early as the evidence supports. The value of this conversation decays to nothing the closer you get to the date."

What they're really checking
  • Do you know which corners are safe to cut and which aren't?
  • Do you bring options rather than escalate a problem?
  • Do you raise risk early?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: "we'd work overtime".
  • Red flag: "add more developers".
  • Red flag: "cut testing" without naming the risk that accepts.
Likely follow-ups
  • Which specific things would you never cut, and why?
  • How do you say this to a stakeholder who doesn't want to hear it?
Velocity dropped 30% this sprint and the manager wants an explanation. ⭐ Senior-level
How I'd answer

"My first move is to push back gently on the premise. Velocity is a noisy planning aid for the team, not a productivity measure, and one sprint's movement is well inside normal variance. Comparing one sprint to one sprint is reading noise. I'd look at five or six before treating it as a signal."

"If there is a real trend, the causes are usually mundane: people on support or leave, a large story carried over so its points landed in a different sprint, unplanned production work, or estimates drifting because the reference story changed. Those are accounting artefacts, not performance changes, and I'd say so plainly."

"The things actually worth worrying about look different — cycle time rising while throughput falls, review latency growing, or an increasing share of time on defect fixes. Those point at real problems: an area of the codebase becoming hostile, a bottleneck reviewer, or quality debt turning into rework."

"So I'd answer with flow metrics rather than points, because they're harder to game and they point at a fixable cause. And I'd be direct that if velocity starts being used as a performance target, the number will go up and mean nothing — estimates are the easiest thing in the world to inflate."

What they're really checking
  • Do you understand measurement noise and Goodhart's law?
  • Can you redirect a manager to a better question without being obstructive?
Likely follow-ups
  • What would you measure instead?
  • How would you explain DORA metrics to that manager?
