Software Development Lifecycle

How a vague request becomes a running, supported production system — and what an interviewer expects you to own at each step.

🟡 Intermediate 🔥 Must know ⭐ Senior-level
Quick revision
  • The lifecycle isn't a sequence you pass through once. On a live product all the stages run at the same time.
  • Junior engineers own code. Senior engineers own outcomes: understood, designed, observable, revertible.
  • A defect gets roughly 10× more expensive each stage it survives. That's the entire argument for shift-left.
  • Non-functional requirements are where projects die. Nobody forgets "users can log in". Everybody forgets "at 3,000 concurrent users with p95 under 400ms".
  • Know the DORA four: deployment frequency, lead time for change, change failure rate, time to restore.
  • SDLC questions are checking one thing: have you actually owned something end to end?

Why this section exists 🟢 Beginner

SDLC questions feel like process trivia, so people under-prepare and answer in textbook terms: "requirements, design, development, testing, deployment, maintenance."

That's technically correct and tells the interviewer nothing. A bootcamp graduate says the same thing.

What they're actually probing is whether you've lived through the consequences:

  • Have you shipped something that broke at 2am?
  • Have you discovered a requirement was wrong after building it?
  • Have you argued for a rollback?
  • Have you written a postmortem, or paid down debt you personally created?

Those stories are what separate seven years of experience from one year repeated seven times.

Senior signal

Connect the stages causally, don't list them. Something like: "we cut change failure rate by moving contract tests into PR validation, which let us deploy more often, which shrank each release's blast radius, which is what actually reduced time to restore." Cause and effect, with a metric attached.

The lifecycle as it actually works 🟡 Intermediate

The waterfall diagram survives in textbooks because it's easy to draw. On a real team, in a single sprint, you are:

  • Gathering requirements for next quarter
  • Designing the next epic
  • Developing the current one
  • Supporting last month's release
  • Paying down debt from last year

What a senior does differently at each stage ⭐ Senior-level

This table is worth knowing in substance. It's the backbone of almost every "walk me through how you'd deliver X" question.

StageMid-levelSenior
Requirements Clarifies ambiguous tickets, asks about edge cases Surfaces the missing non-functional requirements. Challenges scope that doesn't serve the outcome. Negotiates a smaller first slice that de-risks the rest.
Estimation Estimates own tasks reasonably Separates unknown work from known work. Proposes a spike to convert uncertainty into information. Gives ranges plus the assumptions that would break them.
Design Follows the existing architecture correctly Writes down the alternatives and why they lost. Picks the boring option deliberately. Spends the design effort on the one decision that's expensive to reverse.
Development Ships correct, tested code Keeps changes small and independently revertible. Adds the telemetry needed to debug it before it ships.
Code review Catches bugs and style issues Reviews what tests can't check: failure behaviour, backwards compatibility, concurrency, migration safety.
Testing Writes unit tests for their code Owns a test strategy: what's verified at which level, what's deliberately not tested, and how the suite stays fast enough that people run it.
Release Follows the deployment process Decouples deploy from release with flags. Defines the rollback trigger before shipping. Makes the change backwards compatible so both versions can run at once.
Operations Responds to alerts Defines the SLOs. Makes alerts symptom-based and actionable. Deletes the noisy ones. Runs postmortems that produce two or three tracked actions.
Maintenance Fixes bugs as assigned Makes debt visible in business terms — "this costs two days a sprint and is implicated in 40% of incidents" — and repays it incrementally alongside features.

The two ideas that justify everything else 🟢 Beginner

Remember this

Everything that looks like process overhead is buying a shorter feedback loop.

The shorter the time between making a decision and learning whether it was right, the cheaper being wrong becomes.

1. Defects get more expensive the longer they live.

  • Caught in a requirements conversation → costs a conversation.
  • Caught in code review → costs a rewrite of a few files.
  • Caught in QA → costs a cycle of the whole team.
  • Caught in production → costs an incident, possibly data repair, and trust.

The "10× per stage" multiplier is unverifiable folklore, but the ordering is unarguable — and it's the ordering that drives decisions. That's why we do shift-left testing, design review and static analysis in the pipeline.

2. Feedback loop length. CI, trunk-based development, small PRs, feature flags, canary releases, observability — every one of them is buying a shorter loop.

Section contents

Cross-cutting interview questions

Walk me through how a feature goes from idea to production at your company.
How I'd answer

"I'd use a specific feature rather than the generic pipeline. Take the last one I owned — per-tenant export limits. It came in as a support escalation: one customer's exports were starving everyone else's. Product wrote it as 'add export limits', but the real problem was noisy-neighbour contention, so before designing anything I pulled two weeks of metrics to check whether limits were even the right lever."

"Then I'd walk the stages, but anchor each one to a decision I made. What the acceptance criteria were and which non-functional requirements I added. What alternatives I weighed and why I chose the boring one. How I sliced it so the risky part shipped first behind a flag. What I tested at which level — and what I deliberately didn't test."

"How it was released: flag off, then one internal tenant, then a percentage rollout, with a specific metric I was watching that would have triggered a rollback."

"And I'd finish in production. What telemetry I added before shipping, what actually happened when it went live, and what I'd do differently. If something went wrong I'd say so — a candidate whose every story ends perfectly either isn't remembering accurately or hasn't owned much."

What they're really checking
  • Do you engage with problems, or only with tickets?
  • Do you think about release and operation, or stop caring at "merged"?
  • Can you compress a big story into a clear narrative? Same skill as writing a design doc.
Weak answers
  • Red flag: reciting the stages with no concrete example.
  • Red flag: the story ends at merge or "QA tested it".
  • Red flag: "product gives us requirements and we build them" — that describes a code-typing role.
Likely follow-ups
  • What if the metric had gone the wrong way at 10% rollout?
  • Who decided the acceptance criteria, and what did you push back on?
  • How long from first commit to production, and what was the bottleneck?
How do you decide how much process a project needs?
How I'd answer

"Process manages two things: the cost of coordination, and the cost of being wrong. So I size it against those."

"Three people on an internal tool with no compliance exposure and instant rollback need almost none — trunk-based development, CI, a shared channel. A payments service where an error moves real money and reversal is manual needs approvals, audit trails, staged rollouts and change records. That overhead is genuinely worth it there."

"The test I apply to any specific practice is: what failure would this have caught in the last six months, and what did it cost us in the same period? A mandatory two-reviewer policy that's never caught a defect but adds a day of latency to every change is negative value — it's slowing the feedback loop, which makes the system less safe, not more."

"What I try not to do is import process wholesale because it worked somewhere else. Most heavy process is scar tissue from a specific failure, and it only makes sense if you're carrying the same scar."

What they're really checking
  • Do you treat process as cost/benefit, or as identity ("we're Agile")?
  • Can you justify controls in high-risk domains rather than calling them bureaucracy?
Likely follow-ups
  • Give me an example of process you removed. What happened?
  • What about a regulated environment where you can't remove a control?
Your team ships slowly and everyone blames "technical debt". How would you diagnose it?
How I'd answer

"'Slow' is a symptom with several possible causes, and debt is only one. So I'd measure before agreeing with the diagnosis."

"The cheapest instrumentation is lead time broken into stages. How long does a change sit before review, in review, waiting for a build, waiting for QA, waiting for a release window? Teams that feel slow are very often blocked in queueing, not in coding — and queueing is fixable in weeks, whereas a rewrite isn't."

"Then I'd look at change failure rate and where defects come from. High rework means the problem is upstream — unclear requirements causing built-then-rebuilt work. Long review latency means PRs are too big or reviewers are overloaded. A slow flaky test suite shows up as people batching changes to avoid running it, which increases PR size — a nasty loop worth breaking early."

"If it genuinely is debt, I'd localise it. Debt is rarely uniform; usually 10–20% of modules cause most of the friction. Cross-referencing change frequency against defect density finds them fast. Then I'd attack the highest-traffic painful module incrementally — seams, characterisation tests, refactor — and track whether lead time in that area improves. That evidence is what makes debt work fundable."

What they're really checking
  • Do you measure before prescribing?
  • Do you know delivery speed is usually limited by queues and handoffs, not typing?
  • Can you make an engineering-quality argument in terms a business will fund?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: "we need a rewrite" as an opening move.
  • Red flag: proposing more process to fix slowness.
Likely follow-ups
  • How would you convince a PM to fund three sprints of that work?
  • What if the bottleneck turns out to be one senior reviewer?
