Requirements & Analysis

Where most project failures are actually created — and the habits that catch them while they're still cheap.

🟡 Intermediate 🔥 Must know
Quick revision
  • A requirement says what must be true, not how to build it. If it names a technology, it's probably a design decision in disguise.
  • Functional = what the system does. Non-functional = how well. NFRs are the ones people skip, and they dictate the architecture you can't change later.
  • Every requirement needs something observable. "Fast" isn't a requirement. "p95 under 400ms at 500 rps" is.
  • The most valuable question is why — usually asked three times.
  • Ambiguity has tells: "etc.", "and/or", "user-friendly", "handle", "support", passive voice with no actor.
  • Write down assumptions. A written assumption is a risk someone can disprove in five minutes.

What actually goes wrong 🟢 Beginner

Requirement defects are the most expensive kind, because they survive every downstream quality gate:

  • Code review doesn't catch "we built the wrong thing".
  • Unit tests happily verify the wrong behaviour.
  • QA usually tests against the same misunderstood spec.

So it surfaces when a real user touches it — the most expensive moment possible.

Four failure modes cover most of it:

FailureExample
Missing requirementNobody said what happens when the payment provider times out, so the code does whatever it happens to do
Ambiguous requirement"Show recent orders" — three people, three definitions of recent
Solution stated as requirement"Add a Redis cache" instead of "product page loads in under 500ms" — which forecloses cheaper options like fixing the query
Unstated NFREveryone assumed 10,000 records. It's now 4 million and the design can't survive it

Types of requirement 🟢 Beginner

TypeAnswersExample
BusinessWhy are we doing this at all?Cut support contacts about failed exports by 50% this quarter
UserWhat does a person need to do?An admin needs to see why an export failed without contacting support
FunctionalWhat must the system do?The export detail view shows failure reason, timestamp and retry history

Best practice

Write assumptions down explicitly in the ticket. A documented assumption becomes a testable risk someone can invalidate in five minutes. An assumption in your head becomes a late surprise. In interviews, stating assumptions out loud is one of the cheapest ways to look senior.

The questions that find hidden requirements

Keep this as a standing checklist. These consistently surface what nobody wrote down:

  • Volume and growth. How many today, how many in two years, what's the peak vs the average? (A 10:1 peak-to-average ratio changes the architecture.)
  • Failure behaviour. When the downstream system is down — fail, queue, degrade, or serve stale? Who decides?
  • Concurrency. Can two people act on the same thing at once, and what should win? This one question prevents a whole category of production bug.
  • Data lifecycle. Who can see it, how long do we keep it, must it be deletable on request, does it cross a regional boundary?
  • Correctness tolerance. Must this be immediately consistent, or is a few seconds of staleness fine? This determines whether you can use caching, replicas or async messaging at all — see consistency.
  • Reversibility. If this is wrong, how do we undo it?
  • Definition of success. What measurement in three months tells us it was worth building?

Functional vs non-functional 🔥 Must know

Very common interview question. The weak answer is a definition. The strong answer explains why the distinction matters:

Remember this

A missing functional requirement usually costs you a feature. A missing non-functional one often costs you the architecture.

FunctionalNon-functional
DescribesWhat the system doesHow well, under what conditions
Verified byFeature and acceptance testsLoad tests, chaos tests, security review, audits
Cost of adding lateUsually linear — build the featureOften architectural — may need a redesign
Owned byProduct, with engineering inputEngineering, often only if engineering raises it
ExampleUser can export orders to CSV1M rows in under 5 min without hurting API latency

Concretely: if nobody says the export must handle a million rows, you write it synchronously in a request handler. Finding out later means moving to a background worker, adding storage, changing the API contract and re-testing everything.

The NFR checklist ⭐ Senior-level

Run this against any feature — in real work or in a design interview. Each line should end with a number, not an adjective.

CategoryAskTurned into something testable
PerformanceLatency at which percentile, at what load?p95 < 400ms, p99 < 1s at 500 rps sustained
ThroughputHow many per second/day, peak vs average?2M events/day, peak 900/s for 20 min at 09:00 UTC
AvailabilityWhat uptime, measured how, over what window?99.9% monthly on checkout, excluding scheduled maintenance
ScalabilityWhat grows, and by how much?Tenants grow 5× in 18 months; largest tenant is 40% of volume
DurabilityHow much data may we lose in a disaster?RPO 5 minutes, RTO 1 hour
SecurityWho may see and do what?PII encrypted at rest; admin actions require re-auth
ComplianceWhich regime, and what does it force?GDPR erasure within 30 days; EU data stays in EU regions
ObservabilityHow will we debug this at 2am?Correlation ID end to end; failure reason on a dashboard, not just logs
CostWhat's the budget per unit of work?Under $0.002 per processed document at 1M/month
AccessibilityWhich standard?WCAG 2.1 AA on customer-facing screens
Common mistake

Stating availability without saying what "down" means. 99.9% sounds precise but it's 43 minutes a month — and if you measure it as "the health endpoint returned 200", you can be fully compliant while every customer request fails. Tie availability to something users care about: successful checkouts, not pings.

Writing requirements that survive contact with code 🟡 Intermediate

User stories are a format for a conversation. The value is in the acceptance criteria attached to them.

As a tenant administrator
I want to see why an export failed
So that I can fix the input data without raising a support ticket

Acceptance criteria
  1. Given an export in state Failed
     When the admin opens the detail view
     Then the failure reason, timestamp and row number are displayed.

  2. Given a failure with no user-actionable cause
     When the detail view is opened
     Then a generic message and a support reference ID are shown -
     no stack trace, no internal identifiers.

  3. Given an export with 10,000 rows
     When the detail view is opened
     Then it renders within 1 second at p95.

  4. Failure reasons are retained for 90 days, then purged with the export.

Out of scope
  - Retrying the export from this screen (separate story).
  - Bulk failure reporting.

Three things make this useful rather than ceremonial:

  1. It states the error path, including the security decision not to leak internals.
  2. It carries a non-functional criterion with a number.
  3. It declares what's out of scope — which is how you stop the story quietly doubling during development.

INVEST — use it as a review checklist

LetterMeansSmell if violated
IndependentBuildable without another story landing firstStories that must ship as a batch
NegotiableDescribes need, not implementationStory dictates table names and class structure
ValuableDelivers something observable to someone"Create the DAO layer" — that's a task
EstimableThe team can size itCan't estimate ⇒ unknown work ⇒ needs a spike

"Small" means vertically sliced

A thin path through UI, API, domain and storage that a user can actually exercise. Horizontal slices ("build all the entities", "build all the endpoints") give you weeks of work with nothing demonstrable, and pile up all the integration risk at the end — exactly where you least want it.

Spotting ambiguity ⭐ Senior-level

Reading a spec and immediately naming the three things it doesn't say is one of the most visible senior behaviours in an interview. These are the reliable tells:

TellExampleThe question it forces
Unquantified adjective"fast", "large", "recent", "scalable"How fast, at which percentile, under what load?
Open-ended list"CSV, XML, etc."What exactly is in scope? "etc." isn't implementable
Passive voice, no actor"the order is approved"By whom? With what permission? What if they lack it?
and/or"send email and/or SMS"Which one, when, decided by what rule?
Vague verb"handle", "support", "manage", "process"What are the observable outputs and side effects?
No error pathOnly the happy path describedWhat about timeout, duplicate, partial failure, invalid input?
Implicit uniqueness"look up the customer by email"Can two customers share an email? What if zero match?
Missing time semantics"orders from today"Whose timezone? Created or paid? Including in-flight?

Turning requirements into design input 🔴 Advanced

Three artefacts do most of the work:

1. A domain glossary. Agree what a word means before you name a class after it. If Sales say "account" meaning the company and Support say "account" meaning the login, you'll build that confusion into your schema and live with it for years. (This is ubiquitous language — see DDD.)

2. State transitions. For anything with a lifecycle, write the states and the legal moves between them. Most "impossible" production bugs are illegal transitions nobody enumerated: cancelling a shipped order, refunding twice, activating a deleted tenant.

public enum ExportState { Queued, Running, Completed, Failed, Cancelled }

private static readonly Dictionary<ExportState, ExportState[]> Allowed = new()
{
    [ExportState.Queued]    = [ExportState.Running, ExportState.Cancelled],
    [ExportState.Running]   = [ExportState.Completed, ExportState.Failed, ExportState.Cancelled],
    [ExportState.Completed] = [],
    [ExportState.Failed]    = [ExportState.Queued],   // retry - deliberate and documented
    [ExportState.Cancelled] = []
};

The value isn't the code. It's that writing it forces the question "can a cancelled export be retried?" — which costs a minute here and an incident later.

3. Rules with owners. Separate genuine policy ("VAT applies to EU customers") from accidental current behaviour. Policy belongs in the domain and should be easy to change. Accidental behaviour shouldn't be enshrined in tests.

Handling change 🟡 Intermediate

Requirements change. That's normal. What must not happen is change without visibility.

  • Every PR references a work item; every work item states its acceptance criteria.
  • Non-obvious business rules get a test name that names the rule: Refund_IsRejected_When_OrderOlderThan90Days beats TestRefund2.
  • When a requirement changes mid-flight, record it with the reason. Six months later, "why does it do this?" has an answer — which stops someone "fixing" deliberate behaviour.
Production trap — how scope creep actually arrives

Not as "please double the scope". As five "while you're in there" additions, each individually reasonable.

The defence isn't refusal, it's visibility. Accept the change and state its cost in the same sentence: "yes, that's about two extra days and pushes the integration work into next sprint — do you want it now or as a follow-up?" That moves the decision to the person who owns the deadline.

Key takeaways
  • Ask "why" until you reach the problem, not the requester's chosen solution.
  • Run the NFR checklist on everything. One number per quality expectation.
  • Write acceptance criteria as the tests you'll write. If you can't, it isn't testable yet.
  • Ask "what should happen when…" for at least three failure cases before starting.
  • Slice vertically. A slice that can't ship isn't a story.
  • Price scope changes out loud instead of absorbing them silently.

Common mistakes

  • Accepting a solution as a requirement. "Add a queue" may be right — but ask what outcome it's meant to produce. Sometimes a 20-line fix delivers it.
  • Designing for imagined scale. Ask for real numbers. Engineers routinely build for 100× the load that ever arrives and pay for it in complexity daily.
  • Only capturing the happy path. Most production code is error handling; most specs aren't.
  • Gold-plating. Unrequested capability still has to be maintained, tested, documented and secured forever.
  • Silent assumptions about time and identity. Timezones, DST, clock skew, duplicate emails, "unique" identifiers that aren't.
  • Treating the ticket as the whole truth. It's a pointer to a conversation that already happened without you.

Interview questions

Basic

What's the difference between functional and non-functional requirements?
How I'd answer

"Functional describes what the system does — a user can submit an order, an admin can export a report. Non-functional describes how well it must do it under stated conditions: latency, throughput, availability, security, retention, cost."

"The reason I care practically is cost of change. A missing functional requirement costs you the feature you didn't build. A missing non-functional one often costs you the architecture."

"Concrete example: if nobody says the export must handle a million rows, you write it synchronously in a request handler. Finding out later means moving to a background worker, adding storage, changing the API contract and re-testing everything."

"So on any piece of work I run a short checklist — volume and growth, latency target and percentile, availability expectation, failure behaviour, data sensitivity and retention, and cost."

What they're really checking
  • Do you know the definition (table stakes) and why it matters (the signal)?
  • Do you actively elicit NFRs, or wait to be handed them?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: the definition only, with no consequence attached.
  • Red flag: listing "performance, security, scalability" without ever attaching a measurement.
Likely follow-ups
  • Give me an NFR you added that the business hadn't asked for.
  • How would you test an availability requirement?
  • What's the difference between an SLA, an SLO and an SLI?

Intermediate

A product owner gives you a one-line ticket: "Add search to the orders page." What do you do?
How I'd answer

"I wouldn't start with the design. I'd find out what problem search is meant to solve, because that changes almost everything."

"If users are hunting for one order they already know the ID of, that's a lookup and it's trivial. If they want 'all orders from that customer last week that failed', that's filtering — and the right answer is probably structured filters, not a text box. If they're exploring — 'orders mentioning a returned item' — that's real search, and a completely different technology decision."

"Then the specifics: which fields, prefix or substring or full-text, case and accent sensitivity, and — importantly — must it respect tenant and permission boundaries? It must, and that has to be enforced in the query, not filtered after retrieval."

"Volume is the pivotal question. Substring search over 50,000 rows is a LIKE. Over 50 million it's a full-text index or a dedicated search service, and those have very different operational costs."

"I'd also nail down result behaviour, which is always underspecified: sort order, pagination, what shows on zero results, whether archived orders are included."

"Then I'd agree the thinnest first slice — probably exact ID plus a customer filter, server-side paged — ship it, and use real usage data to decide whether fuzzy search is worth building at all."

What they're really checking
  • Do you interrogate the request, or start designing?
  • Do tenancy and authorization come up as part of the requirement?
  • Do you size the solution to the data volume?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: "I'd add Elasticsearch" as the first sentence — a big operational commitment made before knowing the row count.
  • Red flag: no mention of permissions or tenant scoping. Cross-tenant leaks get introduced exactly here.
Likely follow-ups
  • Now they want fuzzy matching on customer name. Does your answer change?
  • How would you keep a search index consistent with the database?
  • What if the largest tenant has 200 million orders and the smallest has 200?
How do you handle a requirement you think is technically wrong?
How I'd answer

"First I check whether I understand the constraint behind it. 'Technically wrong' often turns out to be 'optimising for something I wasn't told about' — a contractual obligation, a migration deadline, a customer commitment. So I ask what outcome the requirement is protecting."

"If it's still wrong after that, I make the cost concrete rather than arguing in principle. Not 'this is bad practice' but: 'this means every tenant shares one table with no partition key, so at our projected growth the biggest tenant's queries will degrade everyone else's by next spring, and splitting it later needs a migration with downtime. The alternative costs about three extra days now.'"

"Then I'd offer a smaller reversible option if one exists — ship the simple version behind a flag, keep the interface stable so the storage decision stays changeable."

"And if the decision goes against me, I disagree and commit — but I write the risk down with a date. That's not political cover. It's so that when the symptom shows up in eight months, the team can connect it to the decision instead of rediscovering it. The thing I most want to avoid is being quietly right while the organisation pays for it."

What they're really checking
  • Can you influence without escalating, and disagree without becoming an obstacle?
  • Do you translate technical risk into business consequence?
  • Do you accept decisions you lost?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: "I'd just build it the right way anyway."
  • Red flag: "I'd escalate to my manager" as step one.
Likely follow-ups
  • Tell me about a time you were overruled and turned out to be right. What did you do next?
  • What if the person insisting is more senior and wrong in public?

Scenario

Two weeks into a six-week feature, you discover the core requirement was misunderstood.
How I'd answer

"Raise it the same day. The cost grows with every day of building on top of it, and there's no version of this where waiting helps."

"But I'd spend an hour first establishing what's actually true, so the conversation is about facts: what was understood, what's actually needed, what of the existing work still applies, and roughly what the options cost."

"Then I'd bring options, not a problem. Usually: (a) adapt what exists — cheapest if the misunderstanding was about behaviour rather than the model; (b) keep the infrastructure and rewrite the domain logic — realistic when the shape of the data was wrong; (c) start over on this piece, which is sometimes genuinely cheapest, because sunk cost isn't a reason to keep bad foundations."

"I'd also want to know how it happened, without making it a blame exercise. It's usually one of three things: the acceptance criteria meant different things to different people, there was no demo of working software until late, or the person who actually knew the domain was never in the conversation. Each has a cheap fix — a glossary, a mid-sprint demo, inviting the right person. Naming the mechanism is what stops it recurring. 'We'll communicate better' doesn't."

What they're really checking
  • Do you surface bad news early? Single most important trait in a senior engineer.
  • Can you reason about sunk cost rationally?
  • Do you fix the process as well as the symptom?
Weak answers
  • Red flag: "keep going and mention it at the sprint review".
  • Red flag: "we can't throw away two weeks of work" — sunk cost reasoning.
Likely follow-ups
  • What if the deadline is committed to a customer and can't move?
  • How would you have caught this in week one?
Sales wants it configurable per customer, engineering wants one behaviour. How do you resolve it?
How I'd answer

"I'd try to turn it from a preference argument into a data question. How many customers have actually asked, what would they set it to, and are those values clustered? Very often 'configurable' turns out to mean two or three real variants — and then the right answer is a small enumeration, which is dramatically cheaper to build, test and support."

"I'd also make the ongoing cost visible, because it's invisible to whoever is asking. Every configuration point multiplies the state space: more test combinations, more support questions of the form 'it behaves differently for this customer', more bugs that only reproduce under one flag combination, and a migration burden every time the feature evolves."

"The framing I use is that configuration isn't free flexibility — it's deferred decision-making that engineering pays interest on forever."

"Practically I'd look for the middle: one sensible default, with the variation behind a per-tenant setting that isn't exposed in the UI initially. That satisfies the one deal that triggered it without committing the product to a configuration surface. And I'd be explicit that this is a product decision with an engineering cost — my job is to price the options accurately, not to win."

What they're really checking
  • Do you understand configurability as a long-term cost rather than a feature?
  • Can you find an option that de-risks both positions?
  • Do you know which decisions are yours to make?
Likely follow-ups
  • How would you implement per-tenant configuration without polluting the domain logic?
  • When does per-tenant customization become a different product rather than a setting?
