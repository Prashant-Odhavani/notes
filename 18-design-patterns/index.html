Design Patterns

Named solutions to problems that keep coming back. The value is the shared vocabulary and the trade-offs — not the class diagrams.

🟡 Intermediate 🔥 Must know
Quick revision
  • A pattern is a problem + solution + known consequences. Quoting the structure without the problem is the classic weak answer.
  • Patterns are recognised, not chosen. You should spot one in code you already wrote.
  • Modern .NET already gives you several: DI containers do Factory and Singleton, LINQ does Iterator, middleware is Chain of Responsibility, delegates make Strategy a one-liner.
  • The ones you'll genuinely use: Strategy, Decorator, Factory, Adapter, Repository, Mediator, Builder, Chain of Responsibility.
  • Every pattern adds indirection. Ask: what change does this make cheap, and what does it cost me to read until then?

Why they matter — and why they get misused 🟢 Beginner

The real value is communication. Saying "this is a decorator chain" conveys structure, intent and consequences in three words.

The second value is that each pattern comes with documented downsides. You inherit other people's experience of where it goes wrong.

The misuse is well documented too: pattern-driven design, where you pick a pattern and then reshape the problem to fit it. The symptom is a codebase where a two-line operation passes through a factory, a strategy, a decorator and a mediator — and no single file tells you what happens.

Remember this

Start with the forces, not the pattern: "what varies here, what must stay stable, and what does the indirection cost?"

Candidates who start by drawing the class diagram are reciting. Candidates who start with what varies are designing.

Pick the pattern from the problem

The problem is…Look at
An algorithm varies, chosen at runtimeStrategy
Add behaviour around something without changing itDecorator, Proxy
An external API's shape doesn't match yoursAdapter
Construction is complex or depends on runtime dataFactory, Builder
Many handlers, one should deal with the requestChain of Responsibility
Things must react without being coupled to the sourceObserver, domain events
Too many objects talking directly to each otherMediator
Complex query rules reused in several placesSpecification
Reads and writes have different shapes and scaling needsCQRS

Cross-cutting interview questions

Which design patterns do you actually use, and when do you avoid them?
How I'd answer

"The ones I reach for regularly are Strategy, Decorator, Factory and Adapter — and I'd describe them by the problem, not the diagram."

"Strategy when behaviour varies by a runtime value and each variant has real logic — pricing rules, export formats, payment providers. Decorator for cross-cutting concerns like caching, retry and logging, because each concern stays in one class and I can compose them per environment. Adapter whenever I integrate a third-party SDK, so its types don't leak into my domain. Factory when construction depends on runtime data rather than a compile-time type."

"I also use several without naming them, because the framework does. Middleware is Chain of Responsibility, IEnumerable with yield is Iterator, the DI container is doing Factory and Singleton. That matters, because patterns describe structure — they're not things you bolt on."

"Where I avoid them is anything speculative. One implementation and no concrete second case in sight? I write the direct code. I also avoid patterns that hide control flow in a team that isn't familiar with them — a mediator is genuinely useful at a certain size, but in a small service it turns a readable call chain into handlers you have to find by naming convention."

"My trigger is usually the third occurrence. First time I write it directly, second time I notice the duplication, third time I extract — because by then I can see what actually varies."

What they're really checking
  • Is your pattern knowledge applied or recited?
  • Can you say no to a pattern? That's the more informative half.
Weak answers
  • Red flag: listing patterns with textbook definitions and no example from your own work.
  • Red flag: "I use singleton and factory a lot" with no mention that DI replaced most of that.
Likely follow-ups
  • Show me how you'd build a decorator chain in ASP.NET Core DI.
  • Which pattern is ASP.NET Core middleware, and what are its consequences?
Can a codebase have too many patterns?
How I'd answer

"Yes — and in my experience it's a more common failure than having too few."

"Every pattern adds indirection, and indirection has a permanent readability cost paid by everyone who reads the code. The benefit only shows up if the anticipated change actually happens."

"The concrete symptom: you can't answer 'what happens when this endpoint is called?' without following registrations through a container and jumping between six files, none of which contains a complete thought. Stack traces stop being useful. New joiners take months instead of weeks. And — the part people underestimate — changing behaviour gets harder, because the abstraction was designed for a different axis of variation than the one that turned up."

"The way I keep it in check is requiring evidence before abstracting. Duplication is cheaper than the wrong abstraction, because duplication is easy to see and easy to consolidate later. A wrong abstraction gets built on and becomes load-bearing. So I duplicate twice and extract on the third."

"I also try to keep indirection near the boundaries — I/O, external services, cross-cutting concerns — rather than through the middle of business logic, so the domain code reads as a plain description of the rules."

What they're really checking
  • Do you treat readability as a first-class engineering concern?
  • Do you understand the wrong-abstraction cost asymmetry?
Likely follow-ups
  • How do you unwind an over-abstracted area without a big-bang rewrite?
  • What's your rule for introducing an interface?
